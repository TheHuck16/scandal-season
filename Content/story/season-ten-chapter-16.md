### L16.S1 · [T] · Rose (alone)
*Purpose: the year's turning — the Park's year; the estate, the seasons. (Turns: 7)*
> *(She wakes before the house does, and the year's first decision is a small one: the boots. They wait by the garden door where she left them, mud caked on the left toe from yesterday's boundary walk. The leather is cold when she pulls them on. It always is. She has stopped expecting otherwise.)*
> *(The house is still behind her — the lamps out, the stairs creaking under her feet, the hall dark as a held breath. She opens the garden door, and the Park is out there in the half-light, dew silvering the grass, the mist sitting low over the lake. She steps into it, and the air is cold enough to wake her properly.)*
> *(She takes the canal first, the way she always takes it first. The towpath is damp under her boots, and the water lies between the reeds without a ripple, holding what is left of the stars. A moorhen is out early, fussing in the rushes with business it will not explain.)*
> *(She walks the canal's whole length. The straightness of it pleases her — the engineer's pleasure, paid for by men long dead who dug it out with spades. She counts her strides without meaning to. It is the ledger's habit, and she lets the numbers run.)*
> *(The lake next, and the swans are on the water with this year's cygnets trailing behind in a line, grey and earnest. The boathouse door stands open and the covers are half off the boats. The season is getting under way, watched or not.)*
> *(The meadows after, and the grass is high and wet against her skirts, and the field waits for the scythe. She stops at the edge and looks across it — green to the hedgerows, the wind moving through it in long waves, the whole field breathing in its sleep.)*
> *(The woods last, before the climb, and the shade takes her, and the leaf-mould is soft under the boots. The old oaks stand in their ranks. A jay screams in the canopy and nothing answers, which is the woods' entire opinion of morning visitors.)*
> *(She comes back by the boundary's height, and the estate lies below in the first light — the lake, the canal, the meadows, the woods — and her head opens its ledger unasked.)*
> *(Letitia is at the garden door when she comes in, the household book under her arm. The hawk's eyes take in the boots, the mud, the hour. She says nothing, which is the quartermaster's form of approval.)*
> "You're up early, darling." Letitia looks at the boots. "The year's walking?"
> "The year's walking." Rose sits on the step to unlace them. "Every mile of it."

### L16.S2 · [T] · Rose (alone)
*Purpose: the spring — the estate's; the waking, the whole. (Turns: 7)*
> *(The rose garden lies in the morning sun, and the head gardener is already among the canes. He has been at work since before she woke — he is always at work before she wakes — and the beds are weeded, the paths swept, and the whole garden wears the order of a man who has been keeping it for forty years and means to keep it for forty more.)*
> *(She walks the paths between the beds. The Hartwell rose is greening on its canes, and the buds are coming fat, and she takes the particular pleasure in them that she takes in a balanced column: completely, and without reservation.)*
> *(The old man straightens when he sees her and touches his hat.)*
> "Miss." He gestures at the canes with his knife. "The greening, miss. You'll see it properly by June."
> "And the buds, gardener?"
> "Fat as plums, miss." He bends a cane down for her to look. "This one's been tended. You'll know the color when it comes."
> *(She bends to look. The buds are green and tight and absolutely certain of themselves. She does not touch them. That is the gardener's rule, and she respects it the way she respects all rules made by men who know more than she does.)*
> *(The boy is at the far bed with the watering can, and he is watering with particular care — slow, deliberate, the can tilted just so — and there is something new in the care. Pride, perhaps. He sees her, and stands straight, and nearly drops the can recovering.)*
> "Miss." He touches his cap. "The watering, miss. The beds, miss."
> "Steady with it, boy. The roots, not the leaves."
> "Aye, miss." He grins. "The roots, miss. I know the roots."
> *(She walks on through the garden, and the bees are working the early flowers, loud in the still air. The head gardener follows her to the gate — he always follows her to the gate — and he is not a man of many words, and he does not waste the few he has.)*
> "Miss." He touches his hat again. "The rose'll bloom, miss. Tended things bloom."
> "Tended things bloom, gardener."
> "Aye, miss." The rarest smile. "That's the way of it."
> *(She walks back through the Park with the garden behind her, and the greening walks with her, and her head does its ledger-work unasked: the acres, the buds, the year's first entries. The figures sit beside the greening the way the ink sits beside the margin. The account is open, and the year has begun to fill it.)*

### L16.S3 · [T] · Rose (alone)
*Purpose: the lake — the spring's; the water, the whole. (Turns: 7)*
> *(The canal runs straight through the Park like a ruled line, and she walks its length in the morning with the sun low over the water. The towpath is damp under her boots. The reeds stand at the edges, and a moorhen nests in the rushes, and the water is so still it holds the sky without a ripple.)*
> *(She walks it the way she always walks it: exactly, and without hurrying. The straightness pleases her — the surveyor's pleasure — and she thinks of the men who dug it, and the years it took them, and the water that has lain in it ever since, patient and level. Men dug, and women walk what they dug, and the world turns on the two of them.)*
> *(Her boots beat the towpath, and she counts the beats without meaning to — the ledger's habit — and she lets the numbers run the way she lets the water run: steadily, and without interference.)*
> *(The head groom rides the far bank on his morning round. The horse's hooves thud soft on the turf. He touches his cap when he sees her and reins in.)*
> "Miss." He looks at the water. "The canal, miss. Walked it all, miss?"
> "Walking it now, groom. The far bank's firm?"
> "Firm as Sunday, miss." He glances down at the lock. "The lock's holding, miss. No leakage."
> "Good. The year's water in the year's canal."
> "Aye, miss." He rides on. "The whole, miss."
> *(She watches him go — the horse, the rider, the far bank holding them both — and the clouds move in the canal's sky, and the clouds do not hurry, and neither does she.)*
> *(Beyond the second bend a heron stands in the shallows, motionless as a held breath. She stands motionless too, out of respect for the profession. The heron does not catch anything while she watches. It does not seem to mind.)*
> *(At the canal's end she stops at the lock. The water waits at the gates, dark and patient. She puts her hand on the lock timber, and the wood is wet and cold, and the cold runs straight up her arm like a report. She turns for home along the far bank, and the towpath carries her, and the morning goes with her.)*

### L16.S4 · [T] · Rose (alone)

### L16.S9 · ★ KEY DECISION 1/3 · Rose (alone)
*Purpose: ★ KEY DECISION 1/3 — THE ESTATE'S LESSON (what the estate teaches: the wholeness, the ground, or the returning). The year deepens; decide the lesson. (Turns: 3 — the decision)*
> *(The year deepens, and the lesson asks its form. She walks the Park in the morning — the whole of it, the estate entire — and the question walks with her, and sits in her stride, and will not be hurried.)*
> *(The wholeness walks first, the way it always walks first: the Park entire, every acre in a single keeping. She has walked every mile of it — the canal's length, the boundary's height, the lake's edge, the woods' shade — and the miles are in her legs, and the legs remember. She looks down from the boundary's height, and the Park lies below in the morning light like something counted, and she is the one who counted it, and the counting says: one thing, not many.)*
> *(The ground walks second: the soil under the boots, the mud worked into the leather. She stops and looks at her boots, and the towpath dirt and the meadow edge and the wood's leaf-mould are all there, ground in. She thinks of the men who dug the canal, and the years it took them, and the water that has lain in it ever since, patient and level. The ground holds everything, and everything she has learned started with looking down.)*
> *(The returning walks third: the year come round, the spring arrived again. She has watched it turn — the greening, the blooming, the cutting, the white — and the turning has never once been wrong. The head gardener said it in the winter, with the frost in his beard: the spring'll come. It always comes. The returning says the walking will be worth walking again.)*
> *(Three forms, and each of them true, and each of them argued by the ground itself. She walks the canal's length, and her boots beat the dirt, and the lesson's form will shape the year's memory — what the year taught, and what of it she keeps. The dusk will sit on the lawn early, and not to be hurried, and neither is she.)*
> *(The head gardener is at the beds as she passes, and he touches his hat, and the boy is at the watering with his can. She stands on the boundary's height with the Park below, and the whole of it waits, and she decides the way she decides everything now: firmly, and for good.)*
> *(The game remembers the choice — and the means: wholeness, ground, or returning. The estate's lesson is hers to give.)*
