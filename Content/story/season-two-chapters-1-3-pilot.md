## Chapter 1 — "The Summons"

**Occasion:** The morning after the Last Assembly — a formal call on Lady Agatha Drummond at eleven o'clock. **Preparation ritual:** Morning dress — the armor of respectability, built at Élise's fitting table for a creditor, not a ballroom. **Purse shelf:** ritual pins draw from play earnings, never Crowns.

**What changes for Rose:** Season One's ruin was proved engineered and Rackham was destroyed — but the victory arrives with an invoice. Rose pays it three ways this chapter: in social currency to Drummond, in trust inside her own house, and in pride where Julian Ashcombe is concerned. By the chapter's end she has sponsored a débutante she did not choose, suspected her own household of a leak, and seen the first paper thread — the de Valcourt papers — that leads up the chain.

### L1.S1 · plot beat · Rose Hartwell, Aunt Letitia Hartwell
*Purpose: Open on the morning after the Last Assembly — victory, and the household already counting its cost.*
> "You were magnificent last night," Letitia said, not looking up from the household book, "and the laundry bill has tripled."
> Rose stood in the doorway of the morning room in her dressing gown, hair still pinned from the night before, and regarded her aunt with the weary amusement of a general being presented with the quartermaster's reckoning after the battle. The house smelled of cold wax and spent flowers. Somewhere below, a footman was moving chairs, and each scrape across the parquet sounded like a small, reproachful cough.
> "The Assembly was a triumph, Letitia. Must it be annotated?"
> "Triumphs are the most expensive line in this book." Letitia turned a page with the care of a woman defusing something. "Candles, musicians, the second supper — the one we did not order but could not decline — and Mr. Quill's fee for the affidavit service, which I have marked in red because it frightens me."
> Rose crossed to the window. Hartwell House sat quiet in the September light, the street below going about its business as if no one had nearly lost everything the night before. A crossing-sweeper worked the corner with philosophical patience. A carriage she did not know rattled past without stopping. The ordinary world had resumed, punctually, as though ruin had merely been a rumor it had declined to believe.
> "Rackham is destroyed. The estate is saved. That is what the red ink bought."
> "The estate is saved," Letitia agreed, "and everyone in London knows it, which is precisely the problem." She closed the book. "Before the ruin was proved engineered, we were pitied. Pity is private. Now we are watched. Watching is public, and the public expects to be entertained."
> "By what? By me?"
> "By the price of it. People do not trust a ruin reversed without a fee somewhere." Letitia rose and came to stand beside her, and her voice softened by a fraction. "You did not sleep."
> "I slept," Rose lied, and felt the familiar, childish comfort of the untruth.
> "You never lie well before breakfast. Drink your tea before it does something irreversible." Letitia pressed the cup into her hands. "And Rose — whatever the morning costs, it costs less than last night nearly did. Remember that when the bills come dressed as callers."
> Rose drank. The tea was strong, and faintly bitter, and exactly what the morning required.

### L1.S3 · plot beat · Rose Hartwell, Aunt Letitia Hartwell
*Purpose: Letitia names the new danger — not debt, but audience; Drummond's terms are still withheld, and the ton is watching the Hartwells wait.*
> "Mrs. Nance Bell has already printed," Letitia said at breakfast, folding the Gazette with the deliberation of a woman handling evidence. "Eat your eggs. The news will not improve them, but the eggs will improve the news."
> "The Assembly was yesterday. The paper cannot have—"
> "The paper has. The proprietor misses nothing." Letitia slid the sheet across the cloth, past the marmalade, and tapped the second column with one decisive finger. "Read it. It is not cruel. That is what troubles me."
> Rose read, and read again, and set the paper down with unusual care, as though it were warm. "'Survives.' 'Waits.' It is only a paragraph, Letitia."
> "It is a paragraph that places a price on you in public. The ton does not watch passively, Rose. It prices. And you are, at this moment, the most interesting price in London — the ruin reversed, the engineer of it destroyed by your evidence, the house standing when every bookmaker in the clubs had laid odds against it. And it has settled on a word for how you did it — Dangerous, Magnanimous, or Leverage, whichever you chose." Letitia buttered her toast with the briskness of a woman who had decided not to be alarmed before ten. "Words travel faster than evidence, my dear. Yours will arrive before you do. Do you know what the ton does with an interesting price? It watches it move. And it takes a commission on the movement."
> "Then let them look." Rose reached for the marmalade with more attention than it deserved. "Looking is not billing."
> "No," said Letitia, "but it is the first step toward billing, and you would do well to remember who did the looking before the ruin. Nobody watched us then. Everybody watches us now. The difference is not flattering." She paused. "And there is the matter of Drummond's terms, still unnamed, still outstanding, like a guest who has not yet sent her regrets."
> The toast, Rose thought, tasted of ash and strategy. She ate it anyway, and folded the Gazette herself, and found that her hands were steadier than her appetite.

### L1.S4 · [T] · Rose Hartwell
*Purpose: Texture — Rose alone with the estate papers, feeling the shape of a victory that has not yet been paid for.*
> The estate papers were stacked on the escritoire in order of dread.
> *(System: the morning's quiet — Quill's affidavit copy, the banker's letter confirming the debts struck, the solicitor's draft of the Hartwell settlements.)*
> Rose ran a finger down the spine of the bundle. Every page was a door that had closed behind her, and every closed door had a cost written on the inside in someone else's hand. The ruin had been engineered — proved, printed, believed — and the engineer destroyed at her own choosing. That was the part the ton applauded: the girl who had faced down Rackham in a ballroom and lived to dance the last set.
> The part the ton did not applaud was Drummond's withheld terms. Lady Agatha Drummond had lent the Hartwells the rope of public credit when the ruin was at its worst — her name spoken in the right rooms, her carriage seen at the right door — and Drummond did not lend rope for sport. Somewhere, in some drawer of that formidable lady's, there was a ledger with Rose's name in it, and the entry was not yet written. Rose could feel it, the unwritten entry, the way one feels a draught from a door one cannot see.
> She lifted the banker's letter and read the confirmation again — the debts struck, the settlements drafted — and tried to feel the relief the words were supposed to confer. It came, but thinly, like tea poured through too much water. Victory, she was learning, was not a state. It was a receipt. And receipts, presented at the wrong counter, could be refused.
> "Win first," Rose told the papers. "Pay after." The papers declined to comment, and she stacked them again in their order of dread, and went down to breakfast feeling precisely as wealthy as a woman who has won everything and owns nothing she has not already promised away — to Drummond, to the ton, to the watching street.

### L1.S5 · plot beat · Rose Hartwell, Aunt Letitia Hartwell
*Purpose: The household staff's loyalty frayed — Rose overhears what survival costs below stairs.*
> "They say she sold the silver to do it," came the whisper from the passage, and Rose stopped with her hand on the door of the stillroom, arrested mid-step like a woman caught eavesdropping in her own house — which, she reflected, was exactly what she was.
> "She did not sell the silver," said the second voice — old Marlow, the housekeeper, sharp as a carving knife and twice as certain. "The silver is where it has always been. > "They say the jewels went first. The Hartwell diamonds—"

Below stairs, the victory was already being priced in rumor: the silver sold, the jewels pawned, the house mortgaged twice over, Miss Hartwell's dresses let out by a dressmaker working on credit and hope. The truth — that Rackham's engineered ruin had collapsed under its own evidence — was less interesting than the fiction, and fiction, as Marlow would have been the first to say, traveled faster than the post.

> "I said nothing."
> "Then mind what others hear you almost say." Letitia bit off her thread. "It amounts to the same, once it reaches the passage."

### L1.S6 · [T] · Rose Hartwell, Élise
*Purpose: Texture — Élise at the fitting table, already reading the season's new brief in Rose's posture.*
> Élise arrived before noon with a basket of muslins and the expression of a general surveying a battlefield.
> "The Assembly gown survived," she announced, circling Rose with a critical eye that missed nothing and forgave less. "You did not. Look at you — shoulders like a woman carrying a piano, and a jaw set for a week of disagreeable callers. Sit. No, stand. Stand is better for the carrying of pianos."
> "I am carrying a piano. It is called the Hartwell name."
> "Then we shall dress the pianist." Élise pinned a length of pearl-grey muslin to Rose's shoulder and stepped back, head tilted. "The season has turned, chérie. Last spring you dressed to be believed — every seam a plea, every collar a character reference. This autumn you dress to be paid — or rather, to be seen paying. There is a difference, and the difference is in the collar."
> "What does the collar say?"
> "That you owe nothing and know it." Élise's smile was all professional confidence. "A collar to the jaw says: I have read the ledger and I am not afraid of the total. A collar at the throat says: let us discuss terms like civilized women. An open collar says — " she paused — "things we do not say before noon, and not to creditors."
> Rose studied herself in the glass, the muslin falling in its provisional folds. "And if the ton does not believe the collar?"
> "The ton believes what it is told by cloth, my love. It has no other education." Élise pinned a second length, burgundy this time, and the two fabrics lay side by side like two different arguments. "We shall see whether London believes the collar or the rumor. My money — " she tapped the pearl-grey — "is on the collar. The collar does not gossip. The collar does not leak. The collar merely stands, and lets everyone else do the talking."

### L1.S7 · plot beat · Rose Hartwell, Aunt Letitia Hartwell
*Purpose: Letitia presses the question of Drummond's withheld terms — the one unpaid debt that matters.*
> "She has said nothing," Letitia said, when the tea had gone cold between them and the afternoon had settled into the particular stillness of a house waiting for the post. "One day since the Assembly, and Lady Agatha Drummond has not called, not written, not so much as nodded at you across a room. That silence is not absence, Rose. It is a bill being drafted."
> "She held our credit when no one else would. I am grateful."
> "Gratitude is a currency she accepts, but she sets the exchange rate, and she does not publish it in advance." Letitia set down her cup with a small, definite sound. "You chose to withhold her terms when the ruin fell — your call, your right, and I backed you in it. But a withheld term is not a forgiven term. It is a term accumulating interest in the dark, and the dark is where Drummond does her best arithmetic."
> Rose looked out at the square, where a carriage she did not recognize had stopped at the corner and stood there a moment too long before moving on. "Do you think she will be cruel?"
> "I think," said Letitia, "that she will be exact. Cruelty would be a kindness — it would give you something to fight, something to resent, something to tell Augusta about over tea. Exactness gives you only something to pay." She followed Rose's gaze to the window. "Was that carriage known to you?"
> "No." Rose watched it turn the corner with the particular attention of a woman learning to watch carriages. "It is the third today I have not known."
> "Then London has begun its audit." Letitia rose and rang for fresh tea, briskly, as though bustle could answer uncertainty. "Drink something warm. Whatever Drummond is drafting, it will be presented in daylight, on heavy paper, and you will meet it the way you met the Assembly — standing, and in the right collar."

### L1.S8 · plot beat · Rose Hartwell
*Purpose: Rose's private resolve — she will follow the money up the chain, not merely survive the social price.*
> Alone in the library, Rose opened Quill's memorandum on Rackham's affairs and read it a third time.
> The ruin had been engineered — but engineering has engineers, and engineers have creditors. Rackham's debts had not evaporated with his disgrace; they had scattered, like shot from a fired gun, lodging in émigré notes, in foreign paper, in obligations that predated the Hartwell ruin by decades and would outlast it by longer. Somewhere up that chain was the money's true shape — not the ruin, which had been the symptom, but the system that had made the ruin profitable — and the shape had a name she did not yet know.
> She took up a pen and wrote, in the margin, a single line: *Follow it up.*
> It was not a plan. It was barely a sentence. But it was the first thing since the Assembly that had felt like her own choice rather than the consequence of someone else's — not a reaction to Rackham, not a debt to Drummond, not a performance for the ton. Hers. The pen scratched against the paper with a sound like a small door unlocking.
> The library door clicked — a footman with the afternoon post, letters fanned on the salver like a losing hand. Rose set the memorandum aside, but the line stayed in her head, ticking like the Hartwell clock.
> *Follow it up.*
> Up past Rackham, past the scattered debts, past the émigré notes with their faded continental hands, to whoever had understood, before she was born, that a Hartwell could be ruined at a profit. She folded the memorandum and slipped it into the escritoire's locked drawer — not hidden, exactly, but no longer lying about in the open like an invitation. Letitia was right about houses leaking. The library had ears, the passages had memories, and a single line in a margin was the kind of thing that traveled.
> But it was hers, and she would keep it.

### L1.S9 · plot beat (villain) · Rose Hartwell, Lady Agatha Drummond (letter)
*Purpose: Drummond's summons — a card, a time, no subject; the bill is being presented.*
> The card arrived with the afternoon post, on paper so heavy it could have served as a weapon.
> *(System: the footman presents it on a salver, as if it might detonate. Rose takes it up.)*
> It bore no crest, no seal — only engraved text in a hand of absolute authority:
>
> *Lady Agatha Drummond*
> *At home tomorrow, eleven o'clock.*
> *Miss Hartwell is expected.*
>
> No subject. No please. No reason given, because none was owed. A time, a place, and the full weight of a name that had held the Hartwells' credit through the worst of the ruin — presented on paper that had clearly cost more than the words it carried.
> "Expected," Rose read aloud. "Not invited. Expected."
> Letitia, who had gone very still, set down her embroidery and came to look over Rose's shoulder. "She does not summon people she means to ruin. She ruins them without the courtesy of a warning — a word dropped at the right dinner, a carriage seen turning away from the wrong door. A summons is — if not kind — at least procedural."
> "Procedure for what?"
> "For the naming of the price." Letitia took the card and studied it as one might study a writ, turning it to the light. "Eleven o'clock. A morning call, formal, witnessed by daylight. She means the ton to know you went to her — the hour guarantees the street will be full of callers' carriages. She means you to know that she means it. There is no softness in this card, Rose. There is not even the pretense of softness."
> Rose turned the card over. The back was blank — no hint, no softening, nothing carried over from the engraver's hand. Just the weight of the paper, and the weight of the name, and the small, cold certainty settling in her chest that the three days of silence had been the drafting, and this was the presentation.
> "Then I shall go," she said, "and I shall be dressed for it."
> "You shall," said Letitia, already reaching for the bell to summon Élise. "And the collar, my love, will be to the jaw."

*Purpose: The night before the call — Letitia's last counsel, and the household's held breath.*

### L1.S13 · [D] · Rose Hartwell, Lady Agatha Drummond
*Purpose: ★ KEY DECISION 1/3 — DRUMMOND'S PRICE. The interview: Drummond names her terms — sponsor Miss Honoria Drummond, the dull but deserving niece. Reputation, not money.*

### L3.S34 · plot beat · Rose Hartwell (the ledgers, night)
*Purpose: Rose consolidates the debt chain; the next step names Drummond's door.*
> *(At her desk, by candlelight, Rose rules the final line under Rackham's chain and writes the summary in her clearest hand — the way Quill taught her, years ago, to write a brief: facts first, conclusions last, no adjectives. ### L3.S35 · plot beat · Rose Hartwell (the adjustment)
*Purpose: Drummond's price sharpened — a sly turn: what he wants isn't money; the adjustment arrives shaped like a kindness.*
> *(The second letter comes at breakfast — no, not a letter. A kindness. That is what makes Rose set down her chocolate and read it twice: Drummond has settled the cloth merchant's debt. Paid it. In full. No conditions named, no price stated — only a short note, in the unknown hand, expressing the hope that Miss Hartwell will remember that old debts, like old friends, are best settled quietly.)*
> *(Letitia's warning comes back to her word for word: he will not punish you for it. He will simply adjust the price. It will arrive shaped like a kindness. Well. Here is the kindness: the ugliest station on Rackham's road, paved over smooth. The chain shortened. The town spared a scandal. And the cost — unstated, unstatable — sitting in Rose's chest like a swallowed stone: now she owes him. Not coin. Gratitude. Obligation. The currencies that never appear in ledgers and always come due, with interest compounded in sleeplessness.)*
> *(And underneath the gratitude, the ledger's question, sharper now: is this the deeper purse? Is Drummond the hand behind Cecilia's runner — the reason a woman of Cecilia's income can afford footmen's loyalty by the pair of boots? Or is the kindness merely what it claims to be, and Cecilia merely what she claims to be — a defeated woman buying news out of spite? Rose does not know. That is the nature of sly men: the kindness and the strategy wear the same reasonable face, the way kindness and strategy wore the same ash-grey gown below stairs.)*
> "Aunt." — Rose, finding Letitia in the morning room. "He paid it. The cloth merchant. All of it."

### L3.S39 · Gazette sting · The Gazette
*Purpose: Beat 8 — the Gazette sting; the ton dislikes a vacuum.*
> *(The Gazette arrives with the morning's papers, as it always does — Mrs. Nance Bell's paper, proprietor named on the masthead, judge of taste and narrator of the ton, never a mystery and never to be hunted. Rose opens it over chocolate, and finds herself, as she has found herself all Season, in its elegant, merciless eye — the eye that sees everything and accuses nothing, which is worse than accusation.)*
> *"Miss Hartwell keeps a quiet week. Hiding, or — the ton dislikes a vacuum — hatching?"*
> *(She reads it three times. Hiding, or hatching. The paper does not accuse; it never accuses — it judges, the way a looking-glass judges, by showing. A quiet week. The ash-grey gown. The stool by the dresser. The peas under her fingernails. None of it named, all of it — seen? No. Sensed. The Gazette senses the way hounds scent: a disturbance in the cover, a quiet where there should be noise. Somewhere between the hall and the town, between Cecilia's runner and the tradesmen's gossip, the paper's correspondents picked up the scent of her descent — and Bell, proprietor-only, printed exactly enough to let the ton do the rest.)*
> "Well." — Rose, to the empty morning room. "Hatching, then. Let us call it hatching — and let the ton wonder what, exactly, is under the shell."
> *(She folds the paper and lays it aside, and she does not — will not — wonder who tells Mrs. Bell's correspondents what. The paper is the weather: you dress for it, you do not interrogate it. That is the rule, and she keeps it. But she marks the sting for what it is: the ton has noticed her absence, and nature — ton-nature — abhors the vacuum she has left. Whatever hatches next had better be worth the quiet. She thinks of the ledgers, the river, Ned's redirected watching, Drummond's kindness, Cecilia's unanswered question. Oh, it will be worth it. One way or another, it will be worth it.)*

*Purpose: Beat 9 — Quill's note: "Come. Not to the office. To the chapel."*
