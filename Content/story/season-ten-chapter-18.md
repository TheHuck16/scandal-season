### L18.S1 · [T] · Rose (alone)
*Purpose: the peace's turning — the Dowager's peace; Augusta, the years. (Turns: 7)*
> *(Dawn comes up the east windows of the corridor in long clean bars, and the Park at this hour belongs to nobody — not the servants, not the ton, not even quite to her. Rose walks it in her wrapper with her hair still in its night plait, because some mornings the house needs seeing before it puts its face on.)*
> *(The boards are cool under her slippers. Below stairs a grate is being cleared, the small domestic thunder of the day beginning without her permission. She passes the morning room with its chairs still pushed in crooked from last night's whist, and Augusta's spectacles case sits on the side table like a small deliberate flag: the Dowager was here, and intends to be here again.)*
> *(Seventy-two. The number has been sitting in Rose's head all week the way a new coin sits in the palm — unfamiliar, worth turning over. Augusta at seventy-two takes the stairs a fraction slower and will not be hurried about it. She has opinions about the new footman's cuffs. She beat Letitia at piquet on Tuesday wearing a look of such complete serenity that Letitia accused her of witchcraft, and Augusta said, "My dear, I am far too old for witchcraft," which only made it worse.)*
> *(At the stairhead Rose stops and looks down into the dim gallery, the portraits hooded in shadow, and the whole long project of the place rises up in her — not the money, never the money first, just the sheer daily fact of it kept. The fires lit. The linen aired. The old woman's spectacles waiting on the side table for hands that have earned their slowness.)*
> *(She thinks of the years as Augusta's own private architecture: not built, exactly, but settled into, the way a river settles into its course. There is a peace in the house this morning that has nothing to do with victories. It is the peace of things done properly for a very long time.)*
> *(A maid comes up the back stairs with a can of hot water, sees her, and drops a curtsy so quick it is almost a stumble. Rose nods her on. The chocolate will be ordered, the letters will come, the day will arrange itself. She goes down, slippers silent on the waxed boards, to begin it.)*

### L18.S2 · [D] · Rose, Augusta
*Purpose: Augusta, the peace's — the Dowager, the years; the spectacles, the kept. (Turns: 8)*
> *(The morning room smells of toast and the particular sharp green of the tea Augusta insists on, which Letitia claims could strip varnish. Augusta is already at the table when Rose comes in, spectacles on, the Gazette folded to the correspondence column, a pencil moving down the margin in small exact ticks.)*
> "Child." Augusta does not look up. "Sit. You are doing arithmetic in the doorway again. I can hear it."
> *(Rose sits. The toast rack is silver, old, dented on one corner from the year Letitia dropped it demonstrating a reel step, and nobody has ever let her forget it.)*
> "I wasn't doing arithmetic," Rose says.
> "You were." Augusta turns a page. "It is your face. You have your mother's forehead when you count things, which is to say you have mine. Eat."
> *(Rose eats. The tea is, as advertised, ferocious. Across the table Augusta reads on, pencil ticking, and there is something in the set of her shoulders this morning that Rose has been watching for weeks without naming: an ease. Not rest, exactly. Augusta does not rest. But the particular vigilance of the last years — the woman who held a house together by force of spectacles — has gone out of her like a tide.)*
> "The line is kept, child." Augusta says it suddenly, to the Gazette, without looking up. "I thought you should hear it said plainly, since you are always waiting for the other shoe. There is no other shoe. The line is kept."
> *(Rose looks at her — the white hair dressed close, the hands steady on the pencil — and feels something turn over in her chest, once, like the new coin.)*
> "Then I shall stop listening for it," she says.
> "You won't," says Augusta, turning another page. "But you may stop admitting it." The rarest smile. "More toast."
> *(The toast arrives, brown and buttered to the exact gold Augusta considers morally acceptable. Rose takes it, and the morning settles around them into its familiar shape — the pencil ticking, the tea steaming, the rain of small decisions falling exactly where they should. She thinks, not for the first time, that peace is not the absence of noise. It is noise in its right place.)*

### L18.S3 · [T] · Rose (alone)
*Purpose: the mornings — Augusta's; the spectacles', the kept. (Turns: 7)*
> *(Augusta's mornings have a liturgy, and the house knows it by heart. Half past seven: the chocolate, black, in the Sèvres cup with the hairline crack she refuses to retire. Eight: the Gazette, correspondence column first, then the leaders, then — if Bell has been particularly waspish — read aloud to whoever is nearest with enormous satisfaction.)*
> *(Rose has taken to joining her uninvited, bringing the household book and her own correspondence, and Augusta has taken to pretending this is an imposition. "You rustle," she said on the first morning. "I do not rustle," Rose said. "You rustle with intent." It is now the established joke of the east wing.)*
> *(This morning the liturgy proceeds: the cup, the crack, the pencil. A letter from the Almerias about the autumn music. A bill from the ironmonger, ticked twice. Augusta holds each paper up to the light as though the paper might be lying, which — Rose reflects, watching the spectacles catch the sun — it occasionally is, and Augusta has caught more lying paper than any woman in England.)*
> *(What Rose notices, this particular morning, is the pace. There is no hurry in it. The old Augusta read the post like dispatches from a siege. This Augusta reads it like weather — worth knowing, not worth fearing. She sets the ironmonger's bill aside for Hodges with a small satisfied nod, as one might set aside a well-behaved dog.)*
> "Anything on fire?" Rose asks, not looking up from her own letters.
> "Nothing that won't keep till Thursday." Augusta folds the Gazette with precision. "Child — when you have a moment that is not rustling. Walk with me in the garden before the heat."
> *(It is barely eight. The garden will be all dew and blackbirds. Rose closes the household book.)*
> "I am entirely at your disposal," she says, "and entirely unrustled."
> "We shall see," says Augusta.
> *(The garden at eight is Augusta's private hour, and Rose knows better than to be late for it. The dew will be on the box hedges, the blackbirds performing their territorial operas, the gardener's boy pretending not to watch the Dowager pass. Some liturgies are worth keeping exactly as they are.)*

### L18.S4 · [T] · Rose (alone)
*Purpose: the letters — Augusta's; the line's, the kept. (Turns: 7)*
> *(The writing desk in the morning room is Augusta's true throne — rosewood, scarred along one edge where a toy phaeton was once driven across it at speed, decades ago. The scar has been polished by seventy years of sleeves into something almost decorative. Augusta writes her letters there every morning at nine, whatever the weather, whatever the house, whatever the century is doing.)*
> *(Rose, sorting the estate post at the smaller table, watches the ritual the way one watches weather: the spectacles lowered, the pen tried twice on the blotter, the first line always begun without hesitation. Augusta does not draft. She decides.)*
> *(The correspondence of the Dowager is a small empire. Cousins in Northumberland. A widowed cousin of the Almerias, whose letters are three pages of health and one of malice. The vicar, about the church roof. A former servant's children, educated at Augusta's expense for thirty years, without ever being mentioned where it could be overheard.)*
> *"The line," Augusta said once, sealing a letter with a decisive thumb, "is not kept by sentiment, child. It is kept by postage." Rose wrote that down in the household book under Tuesday, where it remains the finest thing in it.)*
> *(This morning there are eleven letters. Rose counts without meaning to — old habits — and Augusta catches her at it over the spectacles.)*
> "You may stop counting my letters," Augusta says. "They are not going anywhere."
> "I wasn't—"
> "You were." The pen scratches on. "It is your face." A pause, the pen lifted. "Though if you want something to count, one of them is ordained and preaching. Thirty years of postage, and the interest is sound. There. Count that."
> *(Eleven letters. Rose watches the pen move — tried twice on the blotter, never hurried, never hesitant — and thinks about the arithmetic of a life: thirty years of postage, and a boy gets a living, and nobody ever hears about it unless they happen to be sorting the estate post at the smaller table. The line, kept by postage. She writes that down too.)*
> *(Rose writes it in the margin of the household book — the boy, thirty years — where the fine things live. Augusta's pen goes on scratching, unhurried, and the morning room holds its particular quiet: the sound of a line being kept, one letter at a time.)*

### L18.S5 · [D] · Rose, Augusta
*Purpose: Augusta, the line's — the Dowager, the kept; the seventy-two, the peace. (Turns: 8)*
> *(They are in the long gallery after luncheon, the light coming sideways through the tall windows in slabs, dust turning in it like slow snow. Augusta walks the length of it with her stick — she has finally, this spring, consented to the stick, a handsome malacca thing she pretends to despise — and stops before the Ravencroft portraits, the whole long argument of blood in oils.)*
> "The line." Augusta taps the stick once against the floorboards. "Everyone talks about the line as though it were a rope, child. Something you grip." She looks up at her great-grandfather, who glowers down with the confidence of a man who never once doubted his wig. "It isn't. It's a road. You don't grip a road. You keep it passable."
> *(Rose walks beside her, matching the slower pace without seeming to. This is new, this lecturing in galleries; the old Augusta gave orders, not discourses. Rose is not sure which she prefers. She is sure she prefers the woman giving them.)*
> "Passable for whom?" she asks.
> "For whoever comes next." Augusta moves on, stick ticking. "Your mother understood that. She kept the road for you before you knew you needed one." A glance, sideways, over the spectacles. "You are keeping it for someone you haven't met. That is the whole of it. There is no more."
> *(They reach the window at the gallery's end. Below, the drive curves away between the limes, empty and pale in the afternoon.)*
> "It sounds very simple when you say it," Rose says.
> "It is very simple." Augusta pats her hand once, decisively. "It is only difficult. There is a difference, and the difference is the whole of a life. Shall we go in? The tea will be getting ideas."
> *(They walk back along the gallery the way they came, the stick ticking a steady time between the portraits. The great-grandfather glowers after them. Rose finds she is smiling, and does not examine why, and Augusta — who misses nothing — pretends not to notice, which is her form of tenderness.)*
> *(At the gallery's end they turn toward the stairs, and the light has shifted while they walked, the slabs of sun gone amber at the edges. Augusta's pace is unhurried, and Rose matches it without thinking — the way you match your step to someone you intend to walk beside for a long time. The tea, she reflects, can get all the ideas it likes. It will wait.)*

### L18.S6 · [T] · Rose (alone)
*Purpose: the evenings — Augusta's; the peace's, the kept. (Turns: 7)*
> *(Evenings at the Park have settled into a pattern as fixed as the mornings, and Rose finds she loves it with a fierceness that surprises her. Dinner at seven, never late. The drawing room after, the lamps lit in the particular order Hodges prefers. Augusta with her embroidery — she has taken it up again after twenty years, small furious roses on absurdly fine linen — Letitia with the piquet cards, Rose with whatever book is currently losing the argument for her attention.)*
> *(Tonight it is raining, a soft persistent rain that makes the windows into dark mirrors, and the room has the particular snugness of a house that is entirely sure of its roof. The fire ticks. Augusta's needle flashes. Somewhere in the house a clock strikes nine, and nobody looks up, because nine is when the clock strikes and that is all it is.)*
> *(Rose watches her grandmother's hands — spotted now, the knuckles swollen, moving with the same precision they brought to everything for seventy years — and thinks about what the day has actually contained. Nothing. Letters, a walk, luncheon, the gallery. Nothing that would fill a paragraph of the Gazette. And yet the day sits in her like a good meal.)*
> "You are staring, child," Augusta says without looking up.
> "I am admiring your roses. They are coming out remarkably martial."
> "They are roses. They are not required to be peaceful." The needle pauses. "Though I take your point." She holds the linen up to the lamplight, considers it the way she considers everything — as evidence — and nods once. "It will do. Everything, eventually, does. That is the great secret, and you may have it for nothing: it all does, if you keep at it long enough."
> *(The rain goes on. Letitia deals. The fire ticks. Rose turns her page, and does not read it, and is entirely content.)*
> *(Later, much later, Rose will try to describe this evening and fail, because there is nothing to describe. The rain. The fire. The roses, martial on their linen. It was the kind of evening the house had been building toward for ten years without knowing it — and the knowing, when it came, was as quiet as everything else.)*

### L18.S7 · [T] · Rose (alone)
*Purpose: the portrait — Augusta's; the Dowager's, the kept. (Turns: 7)*
> *(The portrait hangs in the small drawing room, where the afternoon light finds it — Augusta at sixty, painted the year before Rose came to the Park, in the grey silk she still calls her good dress. The painter caught her between expressions: not smiling, not severe, the spectacles in her hand, looking at the viewer the way she looks at the household book — as though deciding whether it will do.)*
> *(Rose stands before it with her hands clasped behind her back, the way Augusta stands before things, and considers the woman in oils. Sixty. A widow eleven years. The Park half-ruined and the money gone and the ton circling like weather. And the woman in the portrait looks out with absolute composure, as though ruin were merely a particularly tedious guest.)*
> *"She hated sitting for it," Letitia says from the doorway, making Rose jump. "Three sittings, and she told the fellow his shadows were impertinent. He painted them in anyway. She never forgave him, and she hung it where the light hits it, which tells you everything about my sister."*
> *(Letitia comes to stand beside her, and for a moment neither speaks. The sisters, Rose thinks — the Dowager and the quartermaster, the line and the laughter. Between them they held this house together through years that would have finished most families.)*
> "Did she know?" Rose asks. "At sixty — did she know it would come right?"
> "Good God, no." Letitia is brisk. "Nobody knows, darling. That's the entire trick of it. She behaved as though she knew, which is better, and cheaper, and frightens the creditors more." She tilts her head at the portrait. "Though between you and me, I think the spectacles knew. They've always known."
> *(Rose laughs, and the portrait looks on, composed, spectacles in hand, keeping its counsel the way its subject keeps everything — exactly, and without fuss.)*
> *(She stays a little longer after Letitia goes, studying the grey silk, the set of the shoulders. Sixty. Eleven years a widow. The line, kept, before there was any reason to believe it could be. She straightens her own shoulders without thinking, and goes to dress for dinner.)*
*Animation: Shared.*

### L18.S8 · [T] · Rose (alone)
*Purpose: the years — Augusta's; the peace's, the turned. (Turns: 7)*
> *(The years turn the way the Park's great beech turns its leaves — not all at once, but with a daily, almost insulting gradualness. Rose notices it in the small treacheries: Augusta reaching for the stair rail she once scorned, the spectacles coming off more often to be polished, the afternoon nap that is never called a nap but is taken, daily, at half past three, in the chair by the library fire.)*
> *(She notices it, and says nothing, because there is nothing to say that Augusta does not already know. The Dowager has been counting her own years far longer than Rose has been counting anything. Instead Rose rearranges the house around the fact the way one rearranges furniture around a draft — quietly, without announcement. The stair rail gets a fresh coat of paint. The library chair acquires a footstool. The afternoon's engagements begin, mysteriously, to end by three.)*
> *(Hodges, who has served the house for thirty years and understands everything, understands this too. "The Dowager's chair, miss," he says, appearing with the footstool as though it had always existed. "Thought it wanted company." It is the most words he has volunteered in thirty years.)*
> *(And Augusta — Augusta accepts the footstool, the rail, the mysteriously shortened afternoons, with the air of a general accepting reinforcements she had already requisitioned. She naps at half past three with her embroidery folded in her lap and her spectacles on her nose, and wakes at four precisely, and is never, ever to be told she was asleep.)*
> "I was resting my eyes," she tells Rose, daring her.
> "Of course," Rose says. "Your eyes have earned it."
> "Hmph." But she is pleased. The years turn, and the house turns with them, and nobody — least of all the Dowager — is so vulgar as to comment.
> *(What Rose understands, watching all this quiet rearrangement, is that the house has learned something she is still learning: that love, at a certain point, stops being grand and becomes administrative. The footstool. The rail. The engagements ending by three. Nobody makes a speech about it. The beech turns its leaves, the years turn, and the Park — which has survived everything — survives this too, by the simple expedient of paying attention.)*
*Animation: Shared.*

### L18.S9 · ★ KEY DECISION 1/3 · Rose (alone)
*Purpose: ★ KEY DECISION 1/3 — THE PEACE'S LESSON (what the peace teaches: the line, the keeping, or the returning). The years deepen; decide the lesson. (Turns: 3 — the decision)*
> *(The years deepen — Rose feels it the way you feel a season change: not in any single day, but in the accumulation. The mornings a fraction slower. The opinions a fraction sharper. The peace, settling over the Park like afternoon light over the gallery. And with the deepening comes the question she has been carrying for weeks without quite asking it: what does the peace teach?)*
> *(She takes it to the walled garden in the late morning, the question, and walks it past the white rose — twenty-five years from a twig — and past the rosemary, the garden that remembers. The peace has been Augusta's great demonstration: seventy-two years of Tuesdays, and one morning the house is quiet and the letters are answered and there is nothing left to fight.)*
> *(But what is the lesson, exactly? She turns it the way Augusta turns paper to the light. Is it the line — the blood, the road, kept passable for whoever comes next? Augusta at sixty, a widow eleven years, behaving as though she knew it would come right. The line, kept, before there was reason.)*
> *(Or is it the keeping — the behavior, not the blood? The chocolate at half past seven. The eleven letters. The peas, unmangled. Show up, write the letters, keep the roof on. The tedious, where the line actually lives. The Dowager's peace, practiced daily until it looked like ease.)*
> *(Or is it the returning — the yearly, the cycle? The white rose, flushing again. The Assembly, convened annually. The road, walked in both directions — the dead, the living, the unborn, in conversation through furniture and bindings. The years, returning, and the peace with them.)*
> *(Three shapes, and all of them true, and all of them Augusta. Rose stands before the white rose with the question, and the garden keeps its counsel, and the years deepen around her like tea in the pot. The peace asks its lesson. The lesson will shape the peace's memory.)*
> *(She thinks of Letitia's fourth shape — the enjoying, unwritten, the most important — and smiles, and holds the three shapes up one last time: the line, the keeping, the returning. The peace asks its lesson. The lesson is hers to decide — and whatever she decides, she will have to live inside it, the way you live inside a house: daily, and without fuss.)*
*★ KEY DECISION 1/3 — The peace's lesson:*
- **The line** — *the kept, taught.* The lesson is the line — Augusta's, the kept. (Consequence: the lesson is the kept's — but kepts are held, and the peace was the given.)
- **The keeping** — *the peace, taught.* The lesson is keeping — the Dowager's, the peace. (Consequence: the lesson is the peace's — but peaces are kept, and the line was the year's.)
- **The returning** — *the yearly, taught.* The lesson is returning — the years, the yearly. (Consequence: the lesson is the yearly's — but yearly's are time's, and the peace was the now.)
> *(The choice is made — line, keeping, or returning, as she decides. The peace's lesson is hers, and the keeping of it starts now.)*

### L18.S10 · [D] · Rose, Augusta
*Purpose: Augusta, the lesson's — the Dowager, the taught; the spectacles, the kept. (Turns: 8)*
> *(They are in the stillroom after luncheon, Augusta decanting the last of the lavender water with the care of a woman handling explosives, when Rose brings her the household book — open, pencil ready — and says, "Tell me what the lesson was." Augusta does not look up from the lavender.)*
> "The lesson," she repeats. "Child, I am seventy-two. There have been several."
> "The peace's lesson. The one you'd want kept."
> *(The lavender drips, slow and fragrant. Augusta sets the decanter down with precision and looks at Rose over the spectacles, and something in her face does the extraordinary thing it does — softens without weakening, the way good steel takes an edge.)*
> "Very well." She folds her hands. "The lesson is this: the line is not the blood, though the blood helps. The line is the behavior. Show up. Write the letters. Keep the roof on. Do it when it is tedious, which is most of the time, because the tedious is where the line actually lives. Anyone can be magnificent in a crisis. It is the Tuesdays that finish people."
> *(Rose writes it down — the Tuesdays — underlining it twice.)*
> "And the peace?" she asks. "Where does the peace come in?"
> "The peace is what the Tuesdays buy you." Augusta picks up the decanter again. "Thirty years of Tuesdays, and one morning you wake up and the house is quiet and the letters are answered and there is nothing left to fight. That is the peace. It is not given. It is accumulated." She holds the lavender to the light. "Like interest. Only reliable."
> *(Rose closes the book on that — like interest, only reliable — and helps her grandmother carry the lavender upstairs, and neither of them mentions that they are both a little moved, because some things are better carried than said.)*
> *(Upstairs, the lavender water is shelved in the stillroom cupboard in its ranked blue bottles, each labeled in Augusta's hand — the same hand that writes eleven letters before nine, that beat Letitia at piquet, that held a ruined house together by force of will. Rose looks at the labels a moment before she closes the cupboard. Like interest. Only reliable.)*

### L18.S11 · [T] · Rose (alone)
*Purpose: the garden — Augusta's; the peace's, the walked. (Turns: 7)*
> *(The walled garden is at its best in the late morning, the walls holding the warmth like cupped hands, and Augusta walks it daily whatever the season — slower now, the stick finding the gravel path with a steady tick, but walking it. Rose goes with her when the household book allows, which is most days, because the garden walk has become one of the liturgies.)*
> *(Today the roses are out in force, the late ones, heavy-headed and impossibly pink against the old brick. Augusta stops before the white rose she planted the year Rose was born — twenty-five years, and the bush is taller than either of them now — and touches a bloom with one finger, the way she touches everything she values: briefly, and as though it might burn her.)*
> "The rosemary," Augusta says, "over there, by the gate. For remembrance — a garden should remember things, even if people won't." She moves on, stick ticking. "Though nobody has ever kept the aphids off the standards. Some battles are not worth the winning."
> *(They walk in companionable silence past the herb beds, the bees loud in the lavender. Rose finds herself watching her grandmother's profile — the nose, the chin, the absolute refusal to hurry — and understands, suddenly and completely, that she is walking with the future. Not her own future. The family's. The road, kept passable.)*
> "The Assembly will want the garden open for the supper interval," Rose says. "The ton will trample the box."
> "The box will recover." Augusta does not break stride. "The ton, regrettably, will too. Open the garden, child. A house that won't show its garden has something wrong with its house."
> *(At the garden gate Augusta stops, and looks back along the path they have walked — the box hedges, the roses, the white rose nodding — with the expression she wears for things she has decided to keep. Twenty-five years, of the rose: a twig. She says it the way other women say grace.)*
> *(The white rose nods behind them in the light wind. The walls hold the warmth. They walk on together, unhurried.)*

### L18.S12 · [T] · Rose (alone)
*Purpose: the Assembly — Augusta's; the convened, the Dowager's. (Turns: 7)*
> *(The Hartwell Assembly has settled into its annual rhythm now — the house party, the ball, the morning after with its glorious wreckage of supper rooms — and Augusta attends it the way a monarch attends a coronation: as the source of its legitimacy. She holds court in the small drawing room during the ball itself, receiving the ton in relays, and the ton comes, because to be received by the Dowager at the Assembly is to be stamped genuine.)*
> *(Rose, circulating with the perpetual half-smile of the hostess, keeps finding her way back to that doorway to check. Each time: Augusta enthroned, spectacles gleaming, some terrified debutante being told — kindly, devastatingly — exactly what is wrong with her curtsey. Each time Rose's chest does the small warm thing.)*
> *"One of the Almeria girls," Augusta reports during a lull, accepting a glass of negus. "Curtseys like a closing fan. I told her so. Her mother thanked me." She sips. "The Assembly suits me, child. I am too old for balls and exactly the right age for courts."*
> *(And it is true — the Assembly is Augusta's element in a way the old London seasons never were. There she was a soldier. Here she is an institution. The difference, Rose reflects, watching the next debutante approach with visible trepidation, is that institutions get to enjoy themselves.)*
> *(Later, when the dancing is at its height and the ballroom glows like a held breath, Rose slips away to the small drawing room and finds Augusta holding forth to a circle of dowagers on the subject of the church roof — still, after all these years, the church roof — and she stands in the doorway a moment, unseen, and simply looks: the white hair, the grey silk, the absolute command. The line, convened. The Dowager, in her kingdom.)*
> *(She goes back to the ballroom before she is missed, but the image stays with her all evening, lit from within like the ballroom itself.)*
> *(Near two in the morning, when the last carriage has gone and the candles are being snuffed in their hundreds, Rose finds Augusta in the small drawing room alone, spectacles off, looking — for once, and only for a moment — her full seventy-two years. "Well," the Dowager says, putting the spectacles on. "That was satisfactory." It is the highest praise she has.)*

### L18.S13 · [T] · Rose (alone)
*Purpose: the toasts — Augusta's; the line's, the raised. (Turns: 7)*
> *(The toasts come at midnight, as they always do at the Assembly — the company gathered, the glasses charged, and Augusta rising with the particular deliberateness that means she has something to say and intends to be heard. The room quiets the way rooms do for her now: completely, and at once.)*
> *"The line," Augusta says, lifting her glass. She looks around the supper room — the candles, the faces, the whole glittering apparatus of the ton at its best behavior. "People ask me what it means. It means this." She pauses, and the pause is a professional instrument. "It means we show up."*
> *(A small ripple — laughter, recognition. The Tuesdays, Rose thinks, translated for company.)*
