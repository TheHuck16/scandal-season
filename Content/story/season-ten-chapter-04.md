### L4.S1 · [T] · Rose (alone)
*Purpose: dawn of the eve — the ball tomorrow; the private word, today. (Turns: 8)*
> She wakes before the maid knocks, which tells her everything about the state of her nerves. For a moment she lies still and listens to the house the way she used to listen to the war-room: for what's wrong. Nothing. The quiet is the finished kind, beeswax and cold ash and cut grass through the open casement, and somewhere below a door closes with the soft decision of someone who knows exactly where they're going.
> The day has two halves, and she does the arithmetic the way she always does, lying flat with the coverlet folded under her elbows. Three o'clock: the dragon, the library, the private word Drummond requested in her own hand — the handwriting like a fence. Nine o'clock tomorrow: the ball. Between them, the eve's dress, the final checks, the household moving through its paces like the well-drilled thing it is. She dresses for both halves at once, because that is what the day requires.
> At the window the Park lies in the dawn the way it has lain every morning this week, and she never tires of checking it: the lake flat as a held breath, the walled garden's geometry softened by mist, the drive swept to the color of wet stone. Last night's moon is still up, a pale coin in a blueing sky, and it occurs to her — unbidden, and she lets it stay — that the moon has watched every eve of this house for two hundred years, and this is the first one that belongs entirely to her.
> The maid, when she comes, finds Rose already at the window in her dressing gown, and does the professional thing: pretends this is normal. "The grey, miss? For the morning?" The eve's dress — dove grey, the Park's stone — laid out with the particular care of a woman who understands that today has two costumes and both matter. Rose dresses with the maid's quick hands, the laces, the pins, the plain pearl drops, and does the day's second arithmetic in the mirror: three o'clock, the library, the dragon; half past, tea; the household's final checks between.
> The ball's eve begins. She goes down to meet it.

### L4.S2 · [D] · Rose, Letitia
*Purpose: preparing for the dragon — the household's arrangements; Letitia's battle-stations. (Turns: 8)*
> The morning room has been arranged the way Letitia arranges everything before a battle: with a quartermaster's eye and no survivors. The tea things stand in formation on the low table. Two chairs face each other across it at the exact angle of equals — or enemies, the geometry is the same. A third chair waits at a remove, near the window, where a person could sit and hear everything and be seen by no one.
> "Three o'clock." Letitia adjusts the third chair a quarter-inch and steps back to check the line of it. "The library, as decided — no, don't argue, darling, the library. Neutral ground with your maps on the walls. She'll look at them and remember what happened to the last person who stood in that room against you."
> "Aunt —"
> "Tea at half past. I will be —" Letitia gestures toward the window chair with the air of a woman describing her own funeral — "elsewhere. But close. Shouting distance."
> "You're arranging the exits."
> "I'm arranging everything." The hawk's eyes are grave and, underneath the gravity, entirely fond. "Dragons, darling. I've read about this. The parley before the battle. She'll come in tired and human and ask for terms you already decided, and you'll have to pretend to think. Be —" she stops, and does something rare: she says the true thing plainly — "be yourself, darling. It's the one thing she can't prepare for."
> "The seating," Letitia continues, warming to the campaign, "is the whole of it. You here, facing the door — no, don't argue, the light's better and she'll have the maps at her back. She'll look at them. They all look at them. It's the room where the war was fought, darling, and rooms have opinions." She moves the tea tray a quarter-inch. "And the exits — the terrace doors unlatched, the corridor clear. The appearance of not needing to flee. There's a difference, and dragons know it."
> "You've thought about this."
> "I've thought about nothing else for a week." The hawk's eyes, unrepentant. "It's what I'm for."
> Rose takes the quartermaster's hands, which are cold with the morning and with whatever quartermasters feel before parleys. The morning room holds its light. Three o'clock: the dragon, the library, the private word. Everything else is already decided except the weather.

### L4.S3 · [D] · Rose, Augusta
*Purpose: the Dowager's counsel on the private word — what dragons say alone. (Turns: 8)*
> Augusta's morning room smells of rosewater and ink, and the Dowager is at her desk with the spectacles down and a letter half-finished, exact at seventy-two as at every age she has ever been. She does not rise. She gestures Rose to the chair and finishes the sentence first, because counsel given in a hurry is counsel wasted.
> "She will not threaten." The spectacles lower another fraction. "Threats are for the letters, child. Alone, she will —" a pause, and Augusta chooses the word with a jeweler's care — "she will be human. That is the danger. Dragons are easy to fight. Tired old women are not."
> "Honoria said she's tired."
> "Honoria is right." Augusta sets down her pen and folds her hands, and for a moment the armor is all the way off, which almost never happens. "Agatha Drummond has fought for fifty years — for her place, for her daughter, for her version of the world. She is tired, and she knows you won, and she has come to —" the rarest pause of all — "to arrange the losing beautifully. Let her. It costs you nothing, and it is the only courtesy she has left to give."
> "And if the terms are hers?"
> "The terms are never hers." The spectacles rise. The armor is back. "Whatever she asks, the answer is on your terms. Not hers. Yours. The house is yours, the ball is yours, the acknowledgment will be yours. She comes to the Park, child. People who come to the Park play the Park's game."
> "One more thing." Augusta rises — she does not rise for many people — and goes to the window, the spectacles catching the light. "Honoria will be with her. The daughter is the war's true casualty, child, and she has spent twenty years between the dragon and the world. Whatever happens at three o'clock, remember the girl at the door. She chose to come too, in her way." A pause. "And Rose — when Agatha Drummond is human at you, it will feel like winning. It is not winning. It is the parley. The winning was the ten seasons. Don't let the parley spend it."
> Rose takes the counsel the way she takes everything Augusta gives her: whole, and to be examined later in private. The dragon, human: the danger. The terms: hers. She kisses the Dowager's cheek — rosewater, papery skin, the faintest powder — and goes to prepare the ground.

### L4.S5 · [T] · Rose, Henry
*Purpose: the daily devotion — Henry's morning check; the small things, on the eve. (Turns: 7)*
> He comes with the morning post, as he has come every morning for longer than either of them would admit to counting, and they walk the terrace together while he delivers the last intelligence. The household lists are closed — every name accounted for, every acceptance in, the arithmetic beautiful.
> "The urns held overnight." He reports like a scout returned from the line. "The lanterns are steady. The drive —" he looks down its length, the full pale sweep of it — "the drive is ready, Rose. Whatever today brings —" he stops, and the stop is the whole sentence.
> "The dragon, Henry."
> "The dragon." Unmoved, the soldier's stillness held in close formation. "She's one woman. You're —" another stop, and this one costs him something — "you're the Park. I'll be —" the smallest smile in the world — "elsewhere. But close. Shouting distance."
> "That's what Letitia said."
> "Letitia and I have an arrangement." He offers his arm for the steps, because the terrace is damp and he is Henry. "She arranges the exits. I arrange the lanterns."
> They walk the terrace's length while the post is dealt with — a letter from the Craykes, twice-confirmed, a gracious note from Old Lady Micklethwaite, the last acceptances stacked and filed. Henry carries the post like dispatches, and somewhere in the middle of it Rose understands that this — the morning walk, the post, the terrace — is the thing she fought the war for. Not the ball. The mornings.
> She laughs — the first laugh of the eve, and it startles them both, and then, just as quickly, it doesn't. "The drive is ready," she says. "Whatever today brings." The devotion in the morning is the steadiest thing in the house: the gift of attention, every day, on purpose. Today it stands in shouting distance, and it has never once failed.
> The terrace's flags are damp underfoot, the air smelling of wet stone and the lime trees. A thrush works the border, the gravel lies raked to corduroy, and the morning does its steady work around them. The devotion is not in the words, which are few. It is in the showing up, which is daily, and has never once failed.

### L4.S7 · [T] · Rose, the gardener
*Purpose: the bloom, at its peak — the Hartwell rose, opening wider; Saturday, promised. (Turns: 7)*
> The walled garden at midday is all green heat and the hum of bees, and the Hartwell rose stands at the bed's center like the answer to a ten-season question. The bloom is at its peak — the petals fully open, the color the Park's stone blushing to rose, the scent the garden's whole ten seasons folded into one flower.
> "For tomorrow, miss." The gardener stands back from it the way men stand back from altars, hands clasped, reverent. "It's at its peak. Another day and it'll be past — but tomorrow —" he grins, and the grin is all pride — "tomorrow it's perfect. Roses know, miss. I told you."
> "You did tell me."
> "Ten seasons, miss. This rose has been bred for ten seasons. It knows what it's for." He straightens, soil on his knees, certainty in his spine. "I mind the year we nearly lost the stock — the frost, the spring it snowed in April. Your aunt wanted to give it up." He shakes his head. "You said no. You said roses know what they're for." A beat. "You were right, miss. They do."
> She remembers the frost year. She remembers saying it. She doesn't remember believing it, quite — she remembers deciding to believe it, which is the same thing if you do it long enough. The bloom opens wider in the midday sun, certain, and the ten seasons sit between them like something solid.
> She bends to the bloom and breathes it in — the peak, the perfection, the legacy literal and living, keeping its appointment. Tomorrow the ton sees it: in her hair, in the vase, on the stone, as she chose. Today it opens for no audience but the garden and the gardener and her. ### L4.S11 · [D] · Rose, Lady Agatha Drummond
*Purpose: the private word, spoken — Drummond, human; the acknowledgment, rehearsed. (Turns: 9)*
> The chosen ground, three o'clock. Drummond sits — actually sits, the dragon at rest, the composure settling around her like a cloak put down — and looks at Rose for a long moment. The silence is not a tactic. Rose has been on the receiving end of Drummond's tactical silences for years, and this is a different animal entirely. This is a woman deciding how to begin.
> "The Park." The voice is the dragon's, but quieter, as if the volume has been turned down by agreement. "I drove the long way. Through the cottages. Past the garden wall." A pause, and the famous control does something Rose has never seen it do: it hesitates. "It is finished, Miss Hartwell. Really finished. I have seen houses built for fifty years, and I —" the rarest stop — "I did not think you would do it."
> Rose waits. Dragons, human: the danger. Augusta's counsel, held like a handrail. Let the silence do its work. The clock ticks. A log settles in the grate.
> "Tomorrow," Drummond says, "I will come to your ball. In purple. Before the ton I tried to keep away, I will —" she looks down at her hands, the rings, the fifty years — "acknowledge what was built. It will be clean, Miss Hartwell. No scene. No humiliation — mine or yours." She looks up, and the eyes are direct. "I wanted you to know before the candles are lit. So you can —" a breath, and the dragon does the bravest thing again — "so you can be ready. As I am trying to be."
> "I read the Gazette," Drummond says, and the non sequitur is so human that Rose almost laughs. "Bell's verdict on the eve. The dragon's purple." The rarest smile, dry as dust. "She makes me sound formidable. I am —" a pause — "I am trying to be formidable about this, Miss Hartwell. It is the only way I know to be afraid."

Rose looks at the fifty-four-year-old woman across the table and does not move. The danger is real; it pulls like the tide. "Then we understand each other," Rose says, and reaches for the teapot with a steady hand. "Clean. No humiliation — yours or mine." She pours. "Now tell me the terms as you see them, Lady Drummond, and I will tell you mine."

### L4.S12 · [D] · Rose, Lady Agatha Drummond
*Purpose: the terms, answered — Rose's terms; the parley, concluded. (Turns: 9)*
> "Clean." Rose repeats the word back to her, testing its weight. "No humiliation — yours or mine." She leans forward, elbows on the war-room's table, the maps rolled and watching from the corner. "Lady Drummond — I accept. On my terms."
> "Which are?" The dragon's eyes narrow, but not unkindly. This is the part she understands: terms.
> "That tomorrow is a celebration, not a surrender — yours or mine. That the acknowledgment is of the house, not of me." Rose holds the dragon's eyes and does not look away, because looking away is a kind of surrender and there will be none today. "And that after tomorrow, the war is over. Really over. No letters. No debts. No absence. The ton sees you acknowledge the Park, and then we are —" she chooses the word — "done."
> Drummond regards her for a long moment — the dragon, weighing, the fifty years of fighting doing their final arithmetic. Then, slowly, a nod. "The house." A second nod, slower. "Not you. I can —" and the voice does something almost wry, almost human, almost warm — "I can acknowledge a house, Miss Hartwell. Houses don't gloat."
> "Houses don't," Rose agrees, "but their mistresses might be tempted. I won't be."
> "No letters," Drummond repeats, testing the term. "No debts. No absence." She looks at Rose with the dragon's directness, but the directness has something else in it now — relief, almost. "Twenty years of letters, Miss Hartwell. Do you know what that costs? The writing, the waiting, the —" she stops. "Done. I accept your terms. The house, acknowledged. The war, over." A pause. "You drive a hard parley for twenty-five."
> "I had a good teacher." Rose doesn't say who. She doesn't need to. The maps are in the corner, retired, and the room remembers.
> "Augusta." Drummond says it like a diagnosis. "Of course. The Dowager teaches hard parleys the way other women teach embroidery." She rises, and the parley is concluded, and the strangest thing is that neither of them wants to leave the room quite yet.

Rose walks her to the door. "In ivory. Clean." At the door Honoria has tears in her eyes and pretends she doesn't, which is the family's entire philosophy, and Rose pretends not to see, which is hers.

### L4.S13 · [T] · Rose, Honoria
*Purpose: the daughter, after — Honoria's relief; the family, the war's true casualty. (Turns: 7)*
> Honoria stays after her mother departs — the daughter in the hall, alone with Rose, with the relief coming off her like heat off a stove. The carriage is gone. The house exhales. Honoria stands in the middle of the hall and does not seem to know what to do with her hands.
> "She meant it." The voice is unsteady, and Honoria is furious about the unsteadiness, which makes it worse. "The clean. The acknowledgment.
