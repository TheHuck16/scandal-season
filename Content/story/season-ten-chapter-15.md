### L15.S1 · [T] · Rose (alone)
*Purpose: the year's turning — the household's year; the keepers, the seasons. (Turns: 7)*
> *(She wakes before the house, which is her habit now — the mistress's hour, the one hour nobody else claims. The bedroom is dark and the corridor darker, and she walks them without a candle, the way she has learned to walk everything here: by memory, and by trust.)*
> *(The house at dawn is a held breath. The gallery sleeps under its dust sheets, the saloon's shutters are closed, and the morning room's desk sits clean — Harrow's doing, the steward's nightly round, the inkwell capped, the blotting paper squared. She puts her hand on the newel post at the stairhead, and the wood is cold, and the cold is honest, the way oak is.)*
> *(Below stairs the first sounds: the kitchen range being coaxed awake, the clink of the cook's pans, Mrs. Quick's keys turning in the still-room lock — the immovable's morning, exact to the minute. Rose stands at the top of the servants' stair and listens, and the listening is a kind of ledger. Every sound is an entry.)*
> *(At the east window she stops, and the Park lies below in the first light — the lake, the woods, the rose garden's dark line — and the year's turning is visible from here if you know how to look. She knows how to look. Ten seasons have taught her.)*
> *(The kitchen girl comes up the back stairs with the first tray, and she stops dead when she sees the mistress at the east window — the chocolate slopping in its cup — and bobs so low the tray tilts.)*
> "Miss! I didn't — shall I bring it here, miss? To the window?"
> "Here is perfect." Rose takes the cup before it can spill. "What's your name?"
> "Nell, miss. New from the village, miss."
> "Nell." She drinks — the chocolate scalding, sweet, perfect. "How long have you been up?"
> "Since five, miss. The range, miss — it won't coax itself."
> *(The chocolate warms her hands through the cup. Outside, a thrush starts up on the north wall, arguing with the morning as it does every year, and the lake shows its first silver seam. She drinks slowly. Nobody hurries the mistress at her window. That is the entire point of the hour.)*
> *(The girl hovers, and Rose dismisses her with a nod, and the tray goes back down, and the east window holds the Park's first light. Five o'clock. The keepers' day starts in the dark, and the mistress's starts at the window. Between them, the house.)*
> *(She finishes the chocolate and sets the cup on the sill for Nell to find. The cup cools there. The light moves an inch up the glass.)*

### L15.S2 · [D] · Rose, Mrs. Quick
*Purpose: the keeper, the year's — Mrs. Quick, the household's; the immovable, the turned. (Turns: 8)*
> *(Mrs. Quick does her morning round at seven, whatever the weather, whatever the company, whatever the year. Rose falls in beside her at the still-room door, and the housekeeper accepts the company the way she accepts everything: without comment, and without slowing.)*
> *(They walk the corridor together — the mistress, the keeper — and Mrs. Quick opens doors, checks sills for dust with one gloved finger, straightens a picture that has hung straight for forty years. The finger comes away clean. The picture stays straight. The round continues.)*
> "The west wing, miss." The immovable voice, nearly warm. "The winter's damp got into the sheets. I've had them aired twice."
> "Twice?"
> "Twice, miss." The keys turn in the linen press. "The Assembly's year, miss — the ton will sleep in those beds. They'll not sleep damp."
> *(The linen press opens on its aired ranks — the sheets folded the exact way Mrs. Quick demands, the lavender between them, the whole press smelling of soap and cold air. Rose runs a hand along a folded stack, and it is cool, and dry, and immaculate.)*
> "Has the silver been done?"
> "The silver's done, miss. Twice." The immovable mouth does the extraordinary thing. "The plate that survived — it's polished, miss. For the year."
> *(They reach the morning room — the round's end — and Mrs. Quick stops with her keys in her hand, and does something Rose has never seen: she hesitates.)*
> "Miss." The immovable voice. "The mattresses, miss. I've had the sheets aired twice, miss, but the mattress —" she stops — "the mattress wants turning, miss. It's the damp, miss. It gets in."
> "Then we'll turn the mattresses, Mrs. Quick. All of them."
> "All of them, miss." The keys turn in her hand — the thought being turned over. "The Assembly's year, miss. The ton'll not sleep damp."
> *(It is the longest speech Rose has ever heard from her, and it is entirely about mattresses, and she finds it the most eloquent thing said in the house all year.)*
> *(She goes — the keys, the corridors — and Rose stands in the morning room's light, and the mattresses will be turned, and the ton will sleep dry, and nobody will ever know. That, Rose thinks, is the whole arrangement: the ton sleeps dry, and never knows why.)*

### L15.S3 · [D] · Rose, Harrow
*Purpose: the steward, the year's — Harrow, the household's; the book, the turned. (Turns: 8)*
> *(Harrow waits in the estate office with the year's book open on the desk — the steward's morning, the honest figures. He rises when she comes in, and the scarred gentleman's bow is exact, and the office smells of ink and beeswax and the year's business.)*
> *(She sits, and he stands at her shoulder, and they go through it together — the figures, the names, the year's honest arithmetic. The maids' wages. The grooms'. The cook's. The gardeners'. Every name a line, every line a keeper.)*
> "The wages, Miss Hartwell." He lays the book open. "The tending." He turns a page with the care of a man handling something alive. "The Assembly's year, miss — the ton'll eat, and sleep, and be kept. It costs." He names no figure she hasn't approved.
> "And the honest?"
> "The honest, miss." He closes the book. "Every penny accounted. That's the charter's house."
> *(She looks at him — the scarred face, the exact hands, the forty years of service in the set of his shoulders — and the image comes uninvited: Harrow young, new to the house, learning the books the way she learned them. The house kept him, and now he keeps the house. It is not a thought she says aloud. Harrow would pretend he hadn't heard it.)*
> *(He turns to the last page — the year's totals — and his pen stops, and the dry face does something complicated.)*
> "Miss Hartwell." A pause. "The —" another pause — "the boy, miss. The gardener's boy. His wages, miss —" the dryest — "he's due the man's rate, miss. He's doing a man's work."
> *(The recommendation sits between them like something alive. Rose thinks of the boy with the watering can, the way he touches his cap the way the old man touches his hat, the charter rose watered first. She does not make him wait for it.)*
> "The man's rate, Harrow. From this quarter."
> "From this quarter, miss." He writes it in — the pen scratching. "The honest, miss. Every penny."
> *(It is a small figure in a big book, and the pen makes its little mark, and the boy will get the man's rate, and the book will balance, and nobody will make a speech about it. Which is, Rose thinks, the whole difference between a house and a sermon.)*
> "The Assembly's year, Harrow."
> "The year's, Miss Hartwell." He blots the page. "Accounted."
> *(She leaves him with his pen and his honest figures, and the boy will get the man's rate, and the book will balance, and the blotting paper takes the last of the ink like a full stop.)*

### L15.S4 · [T] · Rose, the cook
*Purpose: the cook, the year's — the kitchen's; the serene, the turned. (Turns: 7)*
> *(The kitchen at eight is the warmest room in the house, and the cook reigns in it the way Augusta reigns in the morning room: absolutely, and without raising her voice. Rose comes down the back stairs, and the cook looks up from the pastry board, and the serene face does the welcoming thing.)*
> "What are we feeding them, Cook? The Assembly's year — the ton'll sup here."
> "The honest, miss." The serene smile. She goes back to her rolling — the pin moving like a metronome. "The home farm's, the garden's, the dairy's. Nothing bought that can be grown, miss. That's the rule, miss. Your rule, miss."
> "My rule." Rose breathes in — the yeast, the butter, the year's first baking. "They'll sup well."
> *(She shapes a roll with one practiced turn of the wrist, and the dough takes the shape the way it has taken it ten thousand times, and Rose finds herself watching it the way other people watch concerts.)*
> "The keepers eat first, miss." The cook looks up. "The kitchen's rule. A hungry keeper's a careless keeper."
> "The hungry keeper, miss —" the serene smile — "is a keeper who leaves, miss."
> *(It is the plainest economics Rose has heard all year, and it comes from the cook, and it is why the kitchen never loses a girl to London. She makes a note of it for the household book, in the margin, where the best things go.)*
> *(The spit-boy eats his roll standing by the range — the keepers first — and the cook watches him with her whole serene attention, and he is trying, very hard, not to look as though the roll is the best thing that has happened to him all week. It probably is.)*
> "The Assembly's year, miss." The cook wipes her hands on her apron. "The ton'll sup. But the keepers, miss —" she stops — "the keepers sup first, miss. That's the rule, miss. Your rule, miss."
> "My rule." Rose takes another half-roll, warm from the board. "The hungry keeper's a careless keeper."
> "And the fed one, miss —" the serene smile — "is yours."
> *(She goes up the back stairs with the bread warm in her hand, and the kitchen hums below her — the range ticking, the pin turning, the boy at the spit — and the whole warm machine of it turns, and nobody is hungry in it.)*

### L15.S5 · [T] · Rose, the gardeners
*Purpose: the gardeners, the year's — the Park's; the tending, the turned. (Turns: 7)*
> *(The head gardener is waiting by the rose bed with the year's plan in his head, and the morning is fine and clear. He touches his hat when she comes down the path, and the boy is already at the watering, and the can glints.)*
> "The Park at its best this year, Miss Hartwell." The old man touches his hat again. He looks along the beds — the rose row, the kept rows beyond. "The Assembly year — the ton will walk this ground, miss. They'll walk it bloomed."
> "The Hartwell rose, and the rest?"
> "The rest, miss." The rarest smile cracks the weathered face. "The rose belongs to the charter, miss — but the lawns, the woods, the lake edge." He gestures with the pruning knife. "The Park'll do the rest itself, miss. It knows the way."

> "Then the Park's future is in the boy's hands."
> "Aye, miss." He touches his hat. "Safe hands, miss."
> *(The boy comes up the path with the watering can, and he stops when he sees the mistress, and touches his cap the way the old man touches his hat — the gesture copied so faithfully it is almost a quotation.)*
