### L14.S1 · [T] · Rose (alone)
*Purpose: the year's turning — the rose's year; the garden, the seasons. (Turns: 7)*
> *(The walled garden in March, and the beds still wearing their winter straw — the Hartwell rose's row standing bare-caned and patient under a sky the color of wet slate. Rose walks it before the household is up, her boots leaving the first prints of the day in the damp path.)*
> *(The gardener's boy is already at work, raking back the mulch with a care that suggests he has been told exactly what hangs on each cane. She watches him a moment from the gate — the small, deliberate movements, the way he checks each bud as he uncovers it, the way the work is really a kind of listening.)*
> *(She counts the way she counts everything now: one year since the rose was budded onto its stock. One year since she stood here and chose the charter's flower. The ledger in her head turns its own page — planted, tended, wintered — and the arithmetic is simpler than any she has ever kept: wait, and watch, and do not rush.)*
> *(She kneels, heedless of the damp, and touches a cane the way Augusta taught her to touch accounts: firmly, and expecting an answer. The cane is alive under her thumb — green beneath the brown, stubborn as the family. She stands, brushes the straw from her skirts, and walks back to the house through the cold, already planning nothing — which is, for her, a kind of luxury.)*
> *(The head gardener comes up the path with the empty barrow, and he stops when he sees her kneeling in the damp, and his face does the complicated thing it does when the mistress is doing the boy's work.)*
> "Miss Hartwell." He touches his hat. "The year's plan, miss, if you've a moment. The manure for the rose bed — the well-rotted, from the home farm — and the new labels. The old ones have weathered past reading."
> "Order the manure. And have the labels cut fresh — boxwood, not pine. Pine rots."
> "Boxwood." He makes a note in his head, where he keeps everything. "And the Assembly's garden party, miss — the ton'll walk this row. Shall I —" he gestures at the bare canes — "shall I make it pretty, miss? Extra mulch, the edges trimmed sharp?"
> "Make it honest, not pretty." She stands, brushing the straw from her knees. "The ton can see a working bed. They've seen enough pretty."

### L14.S2 · [T] · Rose, the gardeners
*Purpose: the spring — the buds, the year's; the tending, the gardeners'. (Turns: 7)*
> *(April, and the head gardener has summoned her the way other men summon a magistrate — with a look across the breakfast room that says this cannot wait. She finds him in the rose bed with the pruning knife in his hand and the Hartwell rose's first buds fattening on the canes like small green promises.)*
> "Look at the graft, miss." He bends a cane toward her with the tenderness of a man handing over a child. "The join's healed proper. You can see where the bud's worked onto the stock — see? Clean. It wintered sound. The straw did its work, and the frost never bit deeper than the mulch."
> *(She looks where he points, and the scar of the grafting is a pale line, closed and firm, the bud above it swollen and certain. Two years ago she stood here and chose the charter's flower. Last spring she watched the boy rake back the straw. Now this.)*
> "How many?"
> "Eleven canes, thirty-four buds. I counted twice." The rarest smile cracks his weathered face. "It's the charter's, miss, so I counted a third time, to be sure."
> *(The boy comes with the new labels — boxwood, cut fresh, the old ones weathered past reading — and a hank of twine. He ties each label to its cane with a knot he checks twice, tongue out, and stands back.)*
> "Eleven canes," he announces. "Thirty-four buds, miss. I counted."
> "Twice?"
> "Three times, miss." He grins. "It's the charter's. To be sure."
> *(The head gardener inspects the boy's knots the way Augusta inspects a settlement, and finds one crooked, and re-ties it himself with the square knot, and the boy watches, and learns. Ten seasons the old man has watched her learn the garden's grammar. Today she is finally fluent.)*
> *(She walks the beds' length with him, and the buds are green and tight and absolutely certain of themselves, the way only young living things can be. The spring is doing what spring does in a walled garden: arriving all at once, everywhere, as if it had never been away.)*
> *(The thrush is arguing on the north wall, making the same case it made last year. She leaves the gardeners to the watering, and the April light does the rest.)*

### L14.S3 · [T] · Rose (alone)
*Purpose: the tending — the garden's; the woman, the tending's. (Turns: 7)*
> *(She comes back at noon with her gardening gloves on — the plain leather pair, not the kid — and the head gardener looks at her for a long moment, and then hands her the trowel without a word.)*
> *(The work is simple and it is not. She weeds the rose bed's edge, her hands in the cold loam, and the soil gets under her nails and up her sleeves, and somewhere in the third row her thoughts stop being a ledger and become something older: the rhythm of the work, the give of the earth, the small clean snap of a weed's root coming free.)*
> "Not too deep, miss." The gardener's boy, hovering. "The rose's roots run shallow. You'll want to —" he mimes the angle — "like that."
> "Like this?" She adjusts the trowel.
> "Yes, miss. Like that." He nods with the authority of fourteen years. "You've the hands for it."
> *(It is the nicest thing anyone has said to her all week. She works the row to its end, and the sun warms the back of her neck, and the Hartwell rose's canes stand over her work like a congregation. She thinks — the image she would never say aloud — of all the paper she has moved in ten seasons: settlements, charters, invitations. This is the first thing she has ever made with her hands that she cannot file. She likes the feeling more than she intends to admit.)*
> *(The midday bell rings from the stables, and the boy brings her water in a tin cup, and she drinks it standing in the row with the soil on her hands and her back aching in the honest way.)*
> "Miss." The boy hesitates. "The blisters — shall I fetch the salve?"
> *(She looks at her palms. The plain leather gloves have not saved her.)*
> "No." She flexes her hands. "Blisters are the receipt, boy. You pay for the garden in skin."
> *(He grins and goes back to his barrow, and she works the last of the row in the noon heat. When the head gardener inspects her weeding he nods — once — the way he nods at everything she does now, and she carries the nod back to the house like a verdict. Luncheon is cold, and Letitia scolds her, and she does not mind in the slightest.)*

### L14.S4 · [T] · Rose (alone)
*Purpose: the summer — the blooming, the year's; the first, the promised. (Turns: 7)*
> *(June arrives the way June always does at the Park — all at once, with the hayfields going gold and the bees working the garden like it owes them money. And in the rose bed, the first color shows: the Hartwell rose's buds swelling, the green giving way at the tip to something deeper, richer, not yet open.)*
> *(She finds the head gardener there before her, which does not surprise her. He is crouching beside the best bud, and he does not look up when she comes, because some moments are too private for looking up.)*
> "The first." He nods at the bud. "It's the promise kept, miss. Days now. Not weeks. Days."
> "It'll open when it's ready, I know. And not a day before."
> "Aye, miss." He straightens, and his knees crack, and he does not care. "Roses are like the ton. They bloom on their own terms."
> *(She laughs, because he said it to her once before, years ago, when the rose was only an idea. The bud is the size of a thumbnail and it is the most important thing in the Park. She bends to it, close enough to see the veins in the petal-tip, and the color there is — she has no word for it, which annoys her, because she has words for everything.)*
> *(She does not touch it. Some things you earn by not touching. The boy brings the watering can and pours with the ceremony of a priest, and the June light does the rest.)*
> *(She walks back to the house through the heat, and the whole garden hums behind her, and the promise of the thing is almost — almost — better than the thing.)*
> *(She comes back at dusk, unable to stay away, and the bud is fuller, the color deeper, the green nearly gone at the tip. The boy is there before her, caught in the act of checking it himself.)*
> "Miss." He goes red. "I was just — the watering's done, but I thought —"
> "You thought you'd keep an eye."
> "Yes, miss." He stands his ground. "It's the charter's, miss. Someone ought to."
> *(She gestures at the path, and they walk the row together, and he tells her about the bees — the hive by the orchard wall, the queen, the honey — with the authority of fourteen years. The bud holds the dusk light. She leaves the boy to his watch and goes in to dress for dinner, and the garden keeps the evening.)*

### L14.S5 · [T] · Rose, Letitia
*Purpose: Letitia, the blooming — the quartermaster, the rose's; the strategy, the flower's. (Turns: 7)*
> *(Letitia finds her in the rose bed at the height of the morning, and the aunt stands at the gate in her garden hat with the household book tucked under her arm like a weapon.)*
> "Darling." The hawk's eyes sweep the beds. "Do you know what it costs to keep a rose bed? I do. I have the figures." She taps the book. "Manure — the well-rotted, four loads from the home farm. Straw for the winter. The boy's wages —" she names a figure that makes Rose blink — "the knife-sharpening, the twine, the boxwood labels."
> "You sound like you're building a case."
> "I am always building a case, darling. That is the whole of being me." The warm look. "And the case here is this: it is worth every penny, which is not a sentence I say often."
> "You say it about the kitchens."
> "I say it about nothing, darling, and you know it." She bends — careful of her skirts — and puts her nose to a bud, and her face does something entirely unguarded. "Oh. Oh, that's going to be magnificent."
> *(Rose watches her aunt breathe in the future, and the household book hangs forgotten at her side. Even Letitia, quartermaster of everything, has been conquered by a bud.)*
> *(Letitia opens the book then and there, balancing it on the gate, and does the arithmetic aloud with her pencil, and underlines the return twice: a flower that exists nowhere else on earth, the ton walking the row at the Assembly, and the Gazette writing it up. She snaps the book shut.)*
> "And it costs less than the kitchens spend on ices in a week." She pats Rose's cheek, leaving a smudge of pencil lead. "A sound investment, darling. The quartermaster has spoken."
> "Darling." The aunt stops at the terrace steps. "When it opens — the first bloom — I want to be here. Not told about it. Here."
> "You shall be."
> "Good." The smudge stays there all through luncheon, and nobody mentions it, which is the household's whole protocol for Letitia. By supper it has faded to a grey ghost, and Rose has decided to leave it there a little longer.

### L14.S6 · [T] · Rose, Augusta
*Purpose: Augusta, the blooming — the Dowager, the rose's; the line, the flower's. (Turns: 7)*
> *(Augusta comes at the hour she always comes to the garden — late afternoon, when the light is honest — and she walks the rose bed's length with her stick and her spectacles and the particular stillness of a woman who has waited a very long time to be unsurprised.)*
> "The bloom." She stops before the best bud. "The rose's." She bends — seventy-two years bending — and looks at it through her spectacles with the attention she once gave to settlements. "The charter's flower —" the voice does something extraordinary, something soft at the edges — "the line's flower, child."
> "Grandmama."
> "I have seen roses come and go in this garden for sixty years, child." She straightens with effort, and Rose moves to help and is waved off. "Your grandfather planted the first Hartwell bed. Your father let it go to briar. Rackham —" a pause — "Rackham would have sold the soil if he'd found a buyer." She looks at the bud. "And now this. The legacy, child. Yearly."
> *(Rose watches the Dowager's face, and Augusta pretends not to be moved, which is the Dowager's entire philosophy of tenderness. But her hand — the old, ringed hand — rests on the cane a moment longer than botany requires.)*
> "It will be beautiful, Grandmama."
> "Of course it will be beautiful, child. It is ours." The spectacles rise. "See that it opens where the ton can see it. A victory no one witnesses is a rumor. A rose no one witnesses is —" the rarest smile — "merely a rose."
> *(They walk back toward the house together, slowly — Augusta's pace, the stick tapping the gravel — and the Dowager talks, which she does rarely, and only in gardens.)*
> "Your grandfather's rose bed stood where the potting shed is now." The stick taps. "The old Hartwell rose — not this one, the one before. Damask. Scented like —" she stops — "like a church, child. He cut one for me every June, without fail, for forty years. Your father let the bed go to briar in a single season. Men." The spectacles lower. "They build, and they neglect, and they call it fate."
> "And we, Grandmama?"
> "We tend, child." The rarest smile. "Mulch, wait, trust. Your gardener's whole philosophy in three words, and sounder than any settlement I ever signed."
> *(They reach the terrace, and Augusta stops, and looks back at the rose bed — the buds, the row, the kept year standing in the afternoon light.)*
> "And Rose —" at the door, without turning — "it will be beautiful. I have seen sixty Junes in that garden. I know."
> *(The door closes. The stick taps down the corridor. The Dowager has spoken, and the garden keeps the verdict.)*

### L14.S7 · [D] · Rose, the gardeners
*Purpose: the autumn — the cutting, the year's; the gardeners', the tending. (Turns: 8)*
> *(October, and the garden has turned the color of old gold. The head gardener is waiting by the rose bed with the pruning knife and the year's reckoning in his face, and the Hartwell rose's canes — spent now, the season's glory gone to hips and leaf-fall — stand ready for their rest.)*
> "Cut back hard, miss." He touches his hat. "The rose wants its sleep, and the sleeping's half the blooming. You know that now."
> "I know that now." Ten seasons of watching have taught her what no book could: the garden keeps a different calendar than the ballroom, and it does not negotiate.
> *(He works with the knife, and the cuts are clean and sure, and the canes fall in neat bundles. The boy gathers them. She holds the basket, because there is no rank in October, only the work.)*
> "Mulch, wait, trust." The old man wipes the blade. "It's the whole of gardening, miss. Mulch, wait, trust."
> *(She carries the basket of cuttings to the compost, and the garden smells of leaf-mold and coming rain. The cuttings go on the bonfire behind the potting shed, and the smoke rises straight in the still air, and the garden smells of burning rosewood — sharp, sweet, final.)*
> *(The straw waits in the cart. They tuck the bed together, neat handfuls, the gardeners' whole philosophy in the doing of it. The rose is asleep. The year has been kept.)*
> *(The basket of hips goes over her arm — the gardener's wife will make syrup — and the boy walks with her to the still-room door, carrying the straw.)*
> "Miss." He hesitates. "Next year — will it be bigger? The blooming?"
> "The head gardener says roses remember the tending."
> "They do, boy." The old man doesn't look up from the straw. "And this one's been tended. You'll see."
> *(She stays till the fire burns low, and walks back to the house through the falling leaves, and the mulch-wait-trust of it settles into her like a lesson she has finally learned, and the bones are content. Behind her, the garden folds itself for winter, and it knows exactly what it is doing.)*

### L14.S8 · [T] · Rose (alone)
*Purpose: the winter — the sleeping, the year's; the garden, the kept. (Turns: 7)*
> *(December, and the walled garden is a study in white. Snow lies on the straw mulch, and the Hartwell rose's canes — pruned, tucked, sleeping — are small dark lines against it, like writing on a clean page.)*
> *(She walks the bed's length alone, her breath making brief clouds in the still air. The garden is at its most honest now: nothing blooming, nothing promised, nothing to be admired. Just the kept ground, and the kept rose, and the kept year.)*
> *(She thinks of the drawer she cannot open by force. The rose is dreaming under the straw — she knows it the way she knows the house's moods, which is to say she knows it without evidence and is never wrong. Somewhere under the snow, the roots are doing the winter's slow work, and no one can hurry them, and no one needs to.)*
> *(A robin follows her down the row, hopeful. She has nothing for it. The garden in winter gives nothing away — it keeps, and that is enough. She stands a long time at the row's end, looking at the sleeping canes, and the cold gets into her boots, and she does not mind.)*
> *(The image comes uninvited, as her real thoughts do: the rose is the one thing she has made that does not need her. The house needs her. The Assembly needs her. The rose only needs the seasons, and the seasons keep their own counsel. She walks back through the snow, and the prints of her boots are the only writing on the page, and the garden keeps its counsel too.)*
> *(The head gardener comes down the path with extra straw on the barrow, and he stops when he sees her, and touches his hat.)*
> "Miss Hartwell. The hard frost last night — I thought the bed might want another layer. The canes are sound, but there's no harm in —" he pats the straw — "in being certain."
> "Be certain." She steps aside. "I'll watch."
> "The dreaming, miss." The old man straightens, brushing straw from his coat. "The rose dreams under there. You can feel it, if you put your hand down. Warm. Like a sleeping dog."
> *(She puts her hand down into the straw, and it is warm — impossibly, against all the cold — and she looks up at him, and he nods, and neither of them says anything, because some things are too true for talking.)*
> *(She leaves him to the barrow, and walks back through the snow, and the warmth stays in her palm all the way to the house, like a kept secret.)*

### L14.S9 · ★ KEY DECISION 1/3 · Rose (alone)
*Purpose: ★ KEY DECISION 1/3 — THE ROSE'S LESSON (what the rose teaches: the patience, the tending, or the returning). The year deepens; decide the lesson. (Turns: 3 — the decision)*
> *(The year turns, and the rose asks its lesson in the plain evidence of the seasons. Rose walks the walled garden in the year's late light, and the bed is mulched and waiting, and the question sits in her the way the dusk sits on the lawn: early, and not to be hurried.)*
> *(The head gardener's boy has left the watering can by the gate, and the labels are all tied square, and the straw lies neat over the sleeping canes. Everything in its place. The garden in late light is the most orderly thing she owns, and she walks it the way Augusta walks an account — slowly, and expecting it to add up.)*
> *(Patience, the gardeners say — mulch, wait, trust, forty years with a pruning knife in the hand. Tending, her own hands say — the blisters, the soil still under her nails from October, the noon rows worked in the leather gloves. Returning, the rose itself says — it came back from winter, and it will come back again, because that is what roses do when they are kept.)*
> *(She thinks of the strongbox drawer she cannot force. She thinks of the ledger, and the boy's grinning arithmetic, and the head gardener's warm straw, and Augusta's ringed hand on the cane. The garden has taught her all three lessons. It does not care which she names first — but the naming matters, because the name will shape how she remembers the year, and the year is the one she is going to keep.)*
> *(The thrush is still arguing on the north wall. The dusk comes on, and the garden holds it, and the rose bed is a dark line under the straw. The question will not be hurried. She stands at the row's end until the light goes entirely, and then she turns for the house, and she decides the way she decides everything now: firmly, and for good.)*
> *(Behind her, the watering can stands by the gate where the boy left it, and the labels stir faintly in the evening air. The garden does not applaud a decision. It simply keeps what is given it, and waits, and trusts, and the lesson — whichever name she has given it — walks beside her up the path like a companion.)*
*★ KEY DECISION 1/3 — The rose's lesson:*
- **The patience** — *the season, taught.* The lesson is patience — the waiting, the season. (Consequence: the lesson is the season's — but seasons are weather's, and the rose is the tended.)
- **The tending** — *the hands, taught.* The lesson is tending — the hands, the gardeners. (Consequence: the lesson is the hands' — but hands are workers', and the rose is the year's.)
- **The returning** — *the yearly, taught.* The lesson is returning — the year, the yearly. (Consequence: the lesson is the yearly's — but yearly's are time's, and the rose is the bloomed.)

### L14.S10 · [T] · Rose (alone)
*Purpose: the second spring — the buds, the returning; the lesson, the kept. (Turns: 7)*
> *(The second spring arrives, and the garden does not bother with ceremony. One morning the straw is raked back, and there are the buds again — green, fat, certain — the Hartwell rose beginning its year as if the winter had been a rumor.)*
> *(She walks the row with the head gardener, and he is trying not to look proud, and failing.)*
> "It remembers, miss." He touches a bud the way other men touch money. "Roses remember the tending. You can see it in the canes. Strong. Eager." He stops, embarrassed by his own poetry. "It remembers."
> *(She bends to the best of them, and it is already showing the year's ambition — the green splitting, the color waiting underneath. The lesson she named in the late light sits in her like a kept promise, and she does not say its name aloud. Names are for choosing, not for talking about. She straightens, and the spring air is full of the garden's business, and the year begins again.)*
> *(The boy runs past with the watering can, late as always, grinning. The thrush is back on the north wall, making the same argument as last year. The year's first bumblebee finds the row by midmorning, and the boy announces it like a herald.)*
> "Miss! The first bee! On the charter's row!"
> *(The head gardener inspects the labels, and finds one tied crooked, and re-ties it himself with the square knot, and the boy watches, and learns. Some instruction is never finished, only repeated.)*
> *(She walks the row once more before going in — the buds, the labels, the bee — and the garden is the proof of whatever she decided. The rose came back because it was kept. She goes in with the spring on her boots, and the breakfast bell is ringing, and everything — for once — is exactly on time.)*
> *(At the terrace door she stops and looks back, the way she has done every spring since she was a girl. The row stands green and certain in the morning light, and the boy is already late with the watering can, and the old man is already finding fault with the knots. Nothing has changed. Everything has changed. She carries the spring into the house on her boots and leaves it in the hall for Mrs. Quick to sweep up, which the housekeeper does with the air of a woman disposing of evidence.)*

### L14.S11 · [T] · Rose, Julian
*Purpose: Julian, the rose — the free man, the flower's; the terrace, the blooming. (Turns: 7)*
> *(She finds him in the rose garden at the hour when the light goes gold — Julian, standing before the Hartwell rose's row with his hands in his pockets, looking at the buds the way he used to look at the terrace: like a man measuring something he is afraid to want.)*
> "The charter's flower." He does not turn. He touches a bud, gently, with one finger. "The only thing here that doesn't know who I was. It only knows who I am now. That's —" he stops — "that's restful, Rose."
> "You come here often?"
> "Most mornings." The unguarded look, the garden's light on it. "The terrace is yours, and the house is yours, and the whole Park is yours —" the ghost of the old smile — "but the rose is neutral ground. It has no opinions about mended gloves."
> *(He takes the mended gloves from his pocket — the gloves, mended years ago, kept ever since — and turns them over in his hands the way he always does when he is thinking about the free life.)*
> "The last of the brother's debts. Paid in March. With money I earned." The direct look. "Your money never touched it — you'll have Quill check, I know. Have him check."
> "I won't."
> "You will." The ghost of the smile again, warmer now. "It's your nature. It's why the rose blooms."
> *(They stand together in the gold light, and the garden holds them the way it holds everything: without judgment. He does not offer his arm. He offers his company, which is rarer, and she takes it.)*
> *(They walk to the terrace together, slowly, and the Park lies below them — the lake, the woods, the whole kept year — and Julian stops at the terrace's edge the way he always does, like a man who has finally been given permission to look. "Free," he says quietly. "The terrace. The gloves. All of it. Nearly." The word hangs in the gold light, and he does not take it back, and she does not ask him to. Nearly is honest. Nearly is further than he has ever got.)*
> *(The gloves go back in his pocket. The light fails. They walk back through the gold light together, and neither of them hurries.)*

### L14.S12 · [T] · Rose, Laurent
*Purpose: Laurent, the rose — the Englishman, the flower's; the unmasked, the blooming. (Turns: 7)*
> *(Laurent finds her at the rose bed the next morning, and he is carrying two cups of chocolate from the kitchens, which he presents with the gravity of an ambassador delivering terms.)*
> "The charter's flower." He hands her a cup. The English, now, entirely — no accent worn like a mask, no polish laid on like varnish. "You speak of it like a person, I know. It is more reliable than most persons."
> "You speak English like you mean it, these days."
> "I do mean it." He drinks. "The garden is the one place in England where I do not have to be French or English. I can just be —" he searches — "the man who brings the chocolate."
> *(She laughs, and he looks pleased in the unguarded way that still surprises them both. Ten seasons of the polished Frenchman, and underneath it all along was this: a man who notices buds, and brings chocolate, and speaks English like he means it.)*
> "I have decided —" he stops, because Laurent never announces decisions; he arrives at them — "that the garden is my favorite room in your house. Do not tell the ballroom. It would be wounded."
> *(He looks at the buds for a long moment, and then he says, in French so perfect it hurts, "Elle sera magnifique" — and then, catching himself, he grins.)*
> "She will be magnificent. In English." He bows to the rose, solemn as a courtier. "Even my French has stopped performing, these days."
> "Your French never performed, Laurent. Only you did."
> "Ah." He considers this. "You are right, and I am annoyed, which means you are very right." The direct look. "The Assembly's garden party — I shall stand by this row and tell the ton about the grafting. In English. Badly. They will be charmed."
> "They will be terrified."
> "Also good." He takes her hand, and his is warm from the chocolate cup, and the morning is simple and complete. The mask is off — permanently, this time. She holds him to it with a look, and the chocolate is finished, and the morning does what mornings do at the Park now: it behaves.)*
