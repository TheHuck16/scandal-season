### L29.S1 · [T] · Rose (alone)
*Purpose: the assembly's turning — the final, the held; the Hartwell, the years. (Turns: 7)*
> *(The assembly's morning begins in the kitchen, an hour before dawn, because the house's day has always begun there and Rose never saw a reason to change it. The bread ovens are already breathing heat into the dark. the cook stands at the great table with flour to the elbow, directing the day's battle the way other generals direct armies — by knowing exactly where everyone is.)*
> *(Rose is not supposed to be here. The mistress of the Hartwell Assembly should be abed, or at her prayers, or at the very least upstairs being dressed. She is here because the kitchen is the one room in the house where nobody performs for her, and this morning she needs one room that isn't performing.)*
> "Miss." The cook does not look up from the pastry. "You're in the way."
> "I'm observing."
> "Observe from the doorway. The venison pies want the table."
> *(Rose retreats to the doorway with her chocolate and watches the machine work. The cook's book lies open, balanced to the penny — the cook does not lie, and neither does the book. The scullery maids move in the practiced pattern of girls who have done this a hundred times and will do it a hundred more. The ice — the cook's war declaration, fulfilled at ruinous effort — waits in the ice-house, and the ices will be the ton's first surprise: Hartwell, in October, serving ice.)*
> *(Hodges appears in the doorway behind her, silent as weather. "The first carriages at ten, miss. The drive is swept. The gravel is raked to the inch." He pauses. "The staff have asked me to convey — " he clears his throat — "that the house is ready, and they are proud." It is the longest speech Rose has ever heard him make. She has to look away.)*
> *(Upstairs, the house wakes in layers — the shutters opening, the fires lit, the state rooms taking the morning. In the ballroom the chandeliers wait under their covers for the evening, and the floor gleams like a held breath. Rose finishes her chocolate and hands the cup to a scullery maid, who takes it as though it were a sacrament. "Thank you," Rose says, and means the whole of it: the bread, the battle, the morning.)*
> *(She walks out through the kitchen yard as the sky pales. On the drive, far off, the first carriage lamp moves through the dark like a small deliberate star. They are coming. The house is ready. The day has begun where it always begins — with bread, and work, and people who know exactly where everyone is.)*

### L29.S2 · [T] · Rose (alone)
*Purpose: the final — the Assembly's; the Hartwell's, the kept. (Turns: 7)*
> *(By ten the drive is a procession. Rose stands at the door of Hartwell Park — her door, her gravel, her raked inch — and receives the ton the way a general receives an allied army: graciously, and on her own ground.)*
> *(The carriages come in the coachman's order of march, spaced to the minute, and Hodges announces each arrival with the gravity of a man reading scripture. Mrs. Almeria Fane is early, as gossips always are — first to arrive, last to leave, and determined to miss nothing. She embraces Rose with the fervor of a woman greeting a long-lost daughter and immediately begins cataloguing the hall.)*
> "My dear Miss Hartwell — the hall! The staircase! And is that — " her eye fixes on the paper roses in the great vase, lumpy and perfect, made by schoolchildren — "how charming, how very — rustic."
> "They're the Hartwell rose, Mrs. Fane. Grown in our garden. Made by our village."
> "Of course they are." Mrs. Fane's smile does not waver. "Everything here is so very — yours."
> *(The old countess arrives at eleven, having not left London in a decade, and descends from her carriage like a ship coming into harbor. She takes Rose's hand in both of hers. "I said I should come," she announces, to no one in particular. "Someone must see it." She looks up at the house, and her face does something complicated. "Your grandmother would not know it," she says quietly. "She would know you." Then she sweeps inside, and Rose has to stand very still for a moment.)*
> *(The earl comes at noon — the wobbler, won — and shakes Rose's hand like a fellow soldier. "The drive's hedges," he says, by way of greeting. "Trimmed within an inch. Your bailiff's doing, I suppose." "Mine," Rose says. "The bailiff merely executes." The earl laughs, genuinely surprised, and follows Hodges in.)*
> *(They keep coming. The duchess for the blue room. The cautious ones, the Drummond-leaning, the ones who waited to see which way the wind blew — and the wind, it turns out, blew toward Hartwell. Letitia presides in the morning room with the list and the letters, ticking arrivals in ink, and every tick is a small battle won in daylight. By one o'clock the board's columns are full, and Letitia looks up with ink on her nose and says, "Every one of them, Rose. They're all here." The house takes the news the way it takes everything: in order, without fuss. But Rose feels it land like a hand on her shoulder.)*

### L29.S3 · [T] · Rose (alone)
*Purpose: the ballroom — the Assembly's; the convened's, the kept. (Turns: 7)*
> *(The ballroom, at three in the afternoon, belongs to the housekeeper. Mrs. Quick moves through it with the air of a field marshal inspecting troops, and Rose — who has inspected troops in her time — follows in her wake and tries not to interfere.)*
> *(The chandeliers are unbagged, and the light falls through them in clean bright pieces. The floor gleams. The flowers are all Hartwell-grown, from the reclaimed walled garden, per the standing order — and the gardener's boy has outdone himself. The great vases hold loose, living arrangements that look like the garden walked indoors and decided to stay. "He does them like the garden," Mrs. Quick says, with the pride of a woman who spotted talent early, "not like a funeral." The boy, summoned, blushes to the ears and is dismissed with a nod that he will remember for years.)*
> *(The musicians arrive at four — a small orchestra, the best that could be had, engaged at ruinous expense and worth every penny. They tune in the gallery, and the sound drifts down like weather: the violins finding each other, the cello clearing its throat, the flute running a scale like a girl showing off. Rose stands in the middle of the empty floor and listens, and the empty ballroom — which refused the future at dawn a fortnight ago — finally allows it. Music, light, the ton. She can imagine it now. It looks like this: the held breath before.)*
> *(Augusta does the tour at five — the Dowager's eye, the establishment's — and finds no fault, which is the highest praise the tour allows. She stops in the center of the floor, looks up at the chandeliers, and says, "Your mother danced here." It is the first time Augusta has said it. Rose waits. "Badly," Augusta adds. "She had no ear. But she laughed through all of it." The Dowager's whole review of the ballroom, and it is enough. They stand together in the light, and the house holds them both.)*
> *(At six the candles are lit — the long taper, the ballroom kindling one by one — and the room takes the evening the way it takes everything: in order, without fuss. Rose walks the enfilade once more, trailing her hand along the chair rail the way she did at dawn a fortnight ago. The house feels finished. Not restored. Finished. She writes it in the household book, in the margin, in pencil: "The ballroom is ready. It is the finest room in England, and it is mine." Then she goes upstairs to dress.)*

### L29.S4 · [T] · Rose (alone)
*Purpose: the convened — the ton's; the Assembly's, the kept. (Turns: 7)*
> *(The drawing rooms fill the way a tide comes in — gradually, then all at once. By seven the house is full of the ton's talk, and Rose moves through it the way she has learned to move through everything: listening.)*
> *(They are talking about her, of course. They always are. In the blue drawing room, a knot of the cautious ones is doing the arithmetic aloud — the ten seasons, the ruin, the restoration — and arriving, audibly, at the wrong sum. "They say the money was — " "My dear, everyone knows — " "But the house — look at the house — " Rose passes with a tray she does not need to carry and hears the sums change as she goes. The house is the argument. The house always wins.)*
> *(In the green drawing room the young people have claimed the corner by the windows, and their talk is simpler: the music, the dancing, the supper to come. One of them — a girl in her first season, all elbows and hope — catches Rose's eye and says, with terrible sincerity, "Is it true you built all this yourself, Miss Hartwell?" "With help," Rose says. "A great deal of help." "But you — " the girl stops, uncertain of the protocol for addressing a legend. "I kept the books," Rose says, and the girl laughs, delighted, as though it were a joke. Letitia, passing, catches Rose's eye and winks.)*
> *(The old ones hold the fireplace in the small drawing room, and their talk is the longest game: who came, who didn't, what it means. "Drummond's coming," one of them says, and the fire crackles into the silence. "So I hear," says another. "Well. Then it's real. All of it." They look at Rose over their teacups, and she looks back, level, and does not hurry past. The convened, convening. The ton, on her ground, doing its sums in her drawing rooms. She lets them. The house can afford it.)*

### L29.S5 · [T] · Rose (alone)
*Purpose: the company — the Assembly's; the whole's, the kept. (Turns: 7)*
> *(The company, at eight, is the whole — and the whole is stranger and finer than any London ballroom has ever held. The duchess for the blue room takes her soup opposite the bailiff's wife, and they discover, to their mutual surprise, a shared opinion of turnips. The old countess holds court by the fire with the village widow — who was invited because the Park is hers too, and who is telling the countess, at length, about the favors — and the countess is listening with the attention of a woman hearing genuine news.)*
> *(The tenants' guard of honor lines the hall — the carpenter's eldest, the blacksmith's granddaughter (curtsy perfect now, practiced to exhaustion), the schoolchildren scrubbed within an inch of their lives — and the ton has to walk past them to reach the ballroom. Some of the London ones look uncertain. The children stare back, unimpressed. It is, Rose thinks, the finest receiving line in England.)*
> *(Mrs. Almeria Fane has attached herself to the old earl and is extracting the agricultural news with the skill of a woman mining for gossip and finding, to her disappointment, only wheat. "And the hedges," she says faintly. "How — nice." The earl, warming to his subject, tells her about the drainage. Mrs. Fane's smile holds. Rose, passing, feels a surge of affection for them both: the gossip and the farmer, convened, talking about ditches.)*
> *(Lavinia is here — glittering, of course, loyal, of course, hers — and she finds Rose by the stairs with the directness of an old campaigner. "Well," she says. "You did it." "We did it," Rose says. Lavinia considers this, then nods once, sharply. "No," she says. "You did it. I merely watched, and placed the occasional bet." She squeezes Rose's hand, hard, and moves on into the crowd — glittering, loyal, hers — and Rose watches her go with something very like love.)*
> *(The musicians tune again in the gallery — the violins, the cello, the flute — and the house takes the sound as its due. The guests, the village, the tenants, the ton. The company is the whole: London and country, duchesses and bailiffs' wives, gossip and wheat. Rose stands on the stairs and looks down at all of it — the held, the whole — and the evening, which has been approaching for ten seasons, finally arrives.)*

### L29.S6 · [T] · Rose (alone)
*Purpose: the party — the Assembly's; the annual's, the kept. (Turns: 7)*
> *(The party finds its temper by half past eight, and the temper is — Rose searches for the word, standing in the doorway of the ballroom — easy. That is the surprise. She expected grandeur, or tension, or the brittle brightness of a London ball. What she gets is ease: the ease of people who have been fed, and welcomed, and given nowhere to perform.)*
> *(It is the house that does it. The house has no corners for whispering — the enfilade runs straight and bright, the drawing rooms open onto each other, the ballroom is one great honest room. A whisper campaign needs shadows, and Hartwell Park, tonight, has none. The ton, deprived of its natural habitat, is forced to be pleasant. Most of them seem relieved.)*
> *(Letitia finds her with two glasses of champagne and the air of a woman delivering dispatches. "The Drummond-leaning ones," she reports, "are leaning the other way. The cautious ones have stopped being cautious. Mrs. Fane has told three people that the paper roses are 'a statement,' and two of them agreed with her." She hands Rose a glass. "Drink. You've been standing in doorways all evening." "I'm observing." "You've observed. Now participate." They drink, and the champagne is cold, and the ballroom is full, and Letitia — who has been the social general of this war for a fortnight — suddenly looks her age, and then younger than it, and then exactly herself.)*
> *(Augusta, enthroned by the fire with the old ones, catches Rose's eye across the room and raises her glass a quarter-inch. It is the Dowager's whole review of the party, and it is enough. The establishment, convened, is enjoying itself. Lady Drummond's carriage is expected at nine — the old power, coming as a guest to the new one's house, reconciled and invited — and the party, full and easy and entirely Rose's, does not wait. It has already begun.)*
> *(Harrow passes with the wine list and a face of profound disapproval that does not reach his eyes. "The good cellar," he murmurs, "is now the empty cellar." "Then it did its job," Rose says. He almost smiles. The house, the party, the evening — all of it held, all of it hers, all of it, for the first time, annual. The word sits in her mind like a seal: annual. Next year, and the year after. The Hartwell Assembly, convened. She writes it nowhere. Some things are too large for the household book.)*

### L29.S7 · [T] · Rose (alone)
*Purpose: the ball — the Assembly's; the annual's, the kept. (Turns: 7)*
> *(At nine the musicians lift their bows, and the ball begins the way balls have begun for a hundred years: with the rustle of two hundred people turning toward the music at once.)*
> *(The first dance is a country dance — Rose's choice, and a deliberate one. No minuet, no stately opening for the establishment's benefit. A country dance: the whole room moving, the tenants' children watching from the gallery with their feet swinging, the duchess for the blue room taking her place in the set opposite the carpenter's eldest with perfect good humor. The floor fills. The music takes the room the way the tide takes the shore — all at once, and as though it had always been there.)*
> *(Rose does not dance the first set. She stands in the doorway with Letitia and watches, and the watching is the point: this is what she built. Not the house — the house is stone and plaster — but this. The room, the music, the people moving together. The bailiff's wife, flushed and laughing, being turned by a London exquisite who is clearly having the time of his life. The blacksmith's granddaughter, permitted one dance, solemn as a judge. Mrs. Almeria Fane, dancing with the old earl, still talking — even dancing, she talks — and the earl, even dancing, listening about the drainage.)*
> "You should be dancing," Letitia says.
> "I am dancing. I'm dancing the whole room."
> "That's not how it works."
> "It's how it works tonight."
> *(The set ends, and the applause is the room's own — hands, feet, the gallery stamping. The musicians grin at each other, and the first violin lifts his bow for the second dance. Rose feels the evening tilt, the way a ship tilts when the wind finds it: the ball is underway, the party is real, and somewhere on the drive, at this very moment, a carriage is arriving with Lady Drummond in it — the establishment's old power, coming to see what the new one built. She straightens her shoulders — the herself, the unadorned, the unperformed — and goes down to meet the music. The dancing goes on without her for one more set. Then, she decides, she will take the floor. The night is hers. The floor can wait its turn.)*

### L29.S8 · [T] · Rose (alone)
*Purpose: the terms — the Assembly's; her own, the kept. (Turns: 7)*
> *(Augusta finds her between the dances, in the small drawing room, and closes the door — which the Dowager never does, and which tells Rose everything before a word is said.)*
> "The terms," Augusta says. "Before she arrives."
> *(Rose sits. Augusta does not. The Dowager stands by the fire with her hands folded, and lays out the evening's law the way she once laid out the rules of engagement for a Season: plainly, and without ornament.)*
> "She will come in gracious. She will find you early — before the supper, while the room is watching. She will say something that sounds like a compliment and is an acknowledgment, and the room will hear it. Your part is to receive it as the equal she has become, and let the room see the two of you as what you are."
> "And if the room expects a battle?"
> "The room will expect what it expects." Augusta's voice is dry as dust. "She was reconciled in daylight, before witnesses, on your terms. She comes tonight because she was invited, as an equal, and she knows the value of an invitation. What she wants now is the manner of her welcome. Give it to her."
> *(Rose looks at her great-aunt — seventy-two, unbent, the establishment's own — and understands, suddenly, the whole of it: Augusta has been fighting this war longer than Rose has been alive, and tonight the Dowager is handing over the command.)*
> "The terms of the house," Rose says slowly. "No precedence fights. No one is slighted. The village guests are guests. The wine is the good cellar. And — " she stops. "And what?" "And I do not chase her. She comes to me."
> "Good." Augusta moves to the door, then pauses. "One more term, and it is mine, not the house's. Whatever she says — however gracious the acknowledgment — receive it as between equals. The ton respects a victor. It reveres a woman who is gracious in her strength." The door opens. The music comes in like weather. Augusta goes back to her throne, and Rose goes back to her ballroom — her terms, her law, her evening. Lady Drummond's carriage is on the drive. The terms are set.)*

### L29.S12 · [T] · Rose (alone)
*Purpose: the shown — the years'; the kept, the witnessed. (Turns: 7)*
> *(The shown — the years' — and the acknowledgment happens the way Augusta said it would: in the open, before witnesses, between equals.)*
> *(They walk the ballroom together — the old power and the new, arm in arm, the room parting before them like water — and Drummond looks. Really looks.
